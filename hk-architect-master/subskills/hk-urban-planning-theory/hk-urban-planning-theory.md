# HK Urban Planning Theory

Ideas that explain Hong Kong urban form. Each idea names the instrument that states it. When the idea has to be measured, open the subskill in the last column. This file does not copy a statutory table. Architectural theory, including critical regionalism, vertical urbanism, and named practices, stays in `hk-design-theory`.

For **which planning idea should guide the scheme**, use `hk-urban-planning-theory`. For other topics, see the routing table below.

## Propositions

| Idea | What it asks the designer to do | Where it is stated | Where to measure it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rail structures the city | Put intensity at stations and let roads complement the railway | HKPSG Chapter 8 | `hk-urban-space-statutory` |
| Compact city, stepped down from the station | Test the highest domestic intensity at a rail station or a major interchange, then step the ratio down with distance and toward sensitive areas | HKPSG Chapter 2 | `hk-spatial-planning` |
| Self-contained new town | Provide infrastructure and community facilities with the housing, as far as the programme allows | New Town Development Programme, from 1973 | `hk-building-programming` |
| Podium-tower as a planning product | The tall building meets the street through a low zone that can hold retail, station, and public space. The low zone is a planning device, not a style | HKPSG Chapter 11, people-centric building design | `hk-urban-space-statutory` for the qualitative test; PNAP APP-101 when the question is the full-site-coverage podium |
| Public view of ridgeline and harbour | The skyline is a public view from named viewpoints, and the harbour edge is a public edge | HKPSG Chapter 11; Harbour Planning Principles | `hk-urban-space-statutory` |
| Link and place | A footpath is both a route and a place. The Through Zone carries the walk. The frontage and the furniture carry the place | HKPSG Chapter 8 and TPDM Volume 6 Chapter 10 | `hk-urban-space-statutory` |

*Sources: HKPSG Chapters 2, 8 and 11; Harbour Planning Principles (27 April 2006); Government fact sheet on new towns.*

## Classic urban-design books

These books train judgement. They are not Hong Kong instruments. Do not cite a width, a block length, a storey height, or a pattern number from them in a submission. When the idea has to be measured, open `hk-urban-space-statutory`. The same gate is in [foreign design handbooks](../../references/hk-foreign-design-handbooks.md).

| Book | Basic method | Hong Kong use |
|---|---|---|
| Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City* (1960) | A legible city is imageable. People assemble the city from five elements: paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks | Name the path (street or rail), the edge (harbour or ridgeline), the district, the interchange node, and the landmark. The ridgeline test itself is in `hk-urban-space-statutory` |
| Kevin Lynch, *Good City Form* (1981) | Judge a city by performance: vitality, sense, fit, access, and control, and then by efficiency and justice | Use the five performance words as a review. Fit and access are measured by Chapter 8 and the lease, not by this book |
| Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) | Street life needs mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of different ages, and enough concentration for people to be present. Safety is eyes on the street | Ask whether the podium and the street keep different uses and different hours on the same frontage. Block length is the Chapter 8 preference, not a figure from this book |
| Gordon Cullen, *The Concise Townscape* | The city is experienced as a walk: serial vision, the feeling of a place, and the content that occupies it | Walk the approach from the station or the pavement and record the sequence. Do not treat a townscape sketch as a planning permission |
| Jan Gehl, *Life Between Buildings* | Activity outdoors is necessary, optional, or social. Optional and social life appear when the space between buildings is worth being in | Design the edge between the podium and the pavement for staying, not only for passing. Weather protection is a Hong Kong condition the book does not settle |
| Jan Gehl, *Cities for People* (2010) | Decide life first, then the space that supports it, then the buildings. The reference speed is a person walking | Fix the public use of the ground before the tower. Footpath width stays in Chapter 8 and the Transport Planning and Design Manual |
| Ian Bentley, Alan Alcock, Paul Murrain, Sue McGlynn, and Graham Smith, *Responsive Environments* (1985) | Seven qualities: permeability, variety, legibility, robustness, visual appropriateness, richness, and personalisation | Use the seven words as a checklist after the outline zoning plan and Chapter 11. Permeability in metres is PNAP APP-152 |
| Christopher Alexander, Sara Ishikawa, and Murray Silverstein, with others, *A Pattern Language* (1977) | A pattern is a recurring problem and a suggested physical relation, from the region down to the construction detail. The book presents patterns as hypotheses | Ask which relationship is missing: a recognisable neighbourhood, a small public square, life at the edge, a building that addresses the street. Do not copy a pattern's dimensions |
| Colin Rowe and Fred Koetter, *Collage City* (1978) | The city is a collage of pieces, read by figure and ground, not one total diagram | Keep the existing street grain and add the new piece against it. Do not erase a district to complete one diagram |
| Maddalena Iovene, Nicholas Boys Smith, and Chanuki Illushka Seresinhe, *Of Streets and Squares* | People spend time in places that are distinctive, finely grained, and faced with active varied frontages, including streets that follow the land | Test the podium frontage for doors, variety, and a grain smaller than one blank wall. This research is not a Hong Kong facade code |
| Urban design reader, archived PDF | An anthology of urban-design essays. The text layer of this file does not yield a contents list | Use it only to find an essay already named in this table. An essay about another city is not a Hong Kong rule |
