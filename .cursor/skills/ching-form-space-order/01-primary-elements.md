# Primary Elements

Source: Francis D.K. Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, third edition, Chapter 1. Rules below are an original paraphrase for generative use, not a reproduction of the text.

Shared geometry vocabulary for this skill and later chapters:

- A **point** is a position with no extent.
- A **line** is a direction plus a length-to-thickness ratio.
- A **plane** is a surface whose normal classifies it as base, wall, or overhead.
- A **volume** is stored twice: `solidMass` (space displaced) and `enclosedVoid` (space held by planes).

### 1 Primary Elements

#### Concept 1: Dimensional Promotion

* **Definition:** Form is generated by extending an element along a direction it does not already occupy. A point becomes a line, a line becomes a plane, and a plane becomes a volume, each step adding one dimension and a new property set.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Point: position only.
  * Line: length, direction, position.
  * Plane: length and width, shape, surface, orientation, position.
  * Volume: length, width, and depth, form and space, surface, orientation, position.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Promote one dimension at a time. Do not jump from a point to a volume without deciding the intermediate line and plane.
  * Each promotion must name the new properties it introduces. A plane is not "a thick line"; it must carry shape, surface, and orientation.
  * The extension direction must differ from the element's own direction. Extending a line along itself only lengthens it.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Store the chain as `point → line → plane → volume` with an explicit promotion vector at each step.
  * Point: `position` as a 3D vector, extent zero. Promote along one vector to a line segment.
  * Line: promote along a second axis, not the line direction, to a planar face.
  * Plane: promote along the surface normal to a solid or an enclosed void.
  * Reject a promotion whose vector is parallel to the element's existing span.

#### Concept 2: Point

* **Definition:** A point marks a position in space. It has no length, width, or depth, so it is static, centralized, and without direction until it is placed in a field or projected into a visible mark.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Conceptually dimensionless. It can mark the end of a line, the crossing of two lines, the corner where lines meet, or the center of a field.
  * At the center of its field it is stable and organizes other elements around itself.
  * Moved off center, it competes with the field and produces visual tension.
  * To be built, it must be projected vertically (column, obelisk, tower). In plan it still reads as a point. A cylinder, a circle, and a sphere keep that same centered reading.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Use a centered point when the field should feel at rest and subordinate to one place.
  * Offset the point when the field should feel charged. The tension is between the point and the boundary, so keep the boundary legible.
  * A vertical marker commemorates or fixes a place in the landscape only if its plan footprint stays small relative to its height.
  * Do not give a point-element a dominant long axis in plan. If the plan figure elongates, it has become a line.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Represent a point as `Vector3` with zero scale. Visual tension = distance from the field centroid divided by the field's mean radius. Near 0 is stable; values approaching 1 are tense.
  * A point-element is a vertical extrusion whose plan bounding box has aspect ratio near 1 (circle, square, or regular polygon).
  * Tag plan symbols that remain point-like after extrusion as `pointElement`, distinct from linear walls.

#### Concept 3: Two Points

* **Definition:** Two points determine a finite segment and, at the same time, a perpendicular axis about which the pair is symmetrical. The segment can be read as part of a longer path; the perpendicular axis can visually outrank the segment itself.
* **Key Properties:**
  * The segment has finite length but belongs to an infinite path through both points.
  * The perpendicular axis may also be treated as infinite and is often the stronger ordering device.
  * Both the segment and the perpendicular axis dominate any other line that passes through only one of the points.
  * In plan, the pair is a gateway: a plane of entry, plus an approach perpendicular to that plane.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Place two uprights when passage from one place to another must be marked. The approach should arrive normal to the line that joins them.
  * If the perpendicular axis should govern the composition, keep the two points symmetrical about it and do not let nearby forms break that symmetry without cause.
  * Treat the segment as finite for the gateway and as infinite when it must align distant elements.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Given points `A` and `B`, set `segment = B - A` and `axis = normalize(perpendicular in the ground plane, or the world-up cross segment when the pair is vertical)`.
  * Gateway test: the entry plane is the vertical plane through `A` and `B`; the approach is a ray along the ground-plane normal of that plane.
  * Dominance: rank `segment` and `axis` above any line incident to only one endpoint. Prefer the longer of the two as the organizing datum when both are candidates.

#### Concept 4: Line

* **Definition:** A line is a point extended. It has length without width or depth, and because it records the path of a moving point it can express direction, movement, and growth.
* **Key Properties:**
  * It joins, links, supports, surrounds, or crosses other elements; it describes the edges of planes; it articulates their surfaces.
  * It is visible only with some thickness, and it still reads as a line while length dominates width. Contour and continuity set its character (taut or slack, continuous or broken).
  * A run of like elements, repeated without a break, is perceived as one line and carries texture.
  * Vertical lines are in equilibrium with gravity, can stand for the upright body, and mark a position. Horizontal lines read as ground, horizon, or rest. An oblique line is a vertical falling or a horizontal rising, and is visually active because it is unbalanced.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Keep length clearly greater than thickness. When thickness approaches length, the figure becomes a plane or a solid.
  * Use a vertical line to fix a place or to stand in equilibrium. Use a horizontal line to calm a composition or to state the ground. Use an oblique line only when movement or imbalance is intended.
  * A dashed or gapped repetition still counts as one line if the gaps are smaller than the repeated parts and the direction is constant.
  * Edges, joints, and frames are lines. Their visual weight, spacing, and direction will texture the surface they cross.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Lineness = length / thickness. Classify as a line only above a chosen ratio (a practical threshold is greater than about 8:1). Below that, reclassify as a plane or a volume.
  * Orientation class from the direction's angle to world-up: vertical within a small cone, horizontal within a small cone of the ground plane, otherwise oblique.
  * A polyline of repeated instances collapses to one line when spacing is regular, gaps are below a continuity limit, and directions stay within a small angle of each other.

#### Concept 5: Linear Elements

* **Definition:** In a building, lines appear as upright markers, as structural members, as an imagined axis, as a path or a long building, and as the joints and frames that draw edges on a surface.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Vertical markers (columns, obelisks, towers) commemorate an event or fix a point in space.
  * Several uprights can outline a transparent spatial field without filling it. Four towers, for example, can bound a volume whose focus sits inside the outline.
  * Linear members with sufficient strength span between supports, carry an overhead plane, or assemble into a three-dimensional frame.
  * An axis is a line that may not be built: two distant points establish it, and elements arrange symmetrically about it.
  * A building or a room becomes linear when it follows a path of movement and links spaces in sequence. A linear building can enclose an outdoor room and can bend to a site.
  * At small scale, joints, opening frames, and column-and-beam grids articulate edges. Texture depends on visual weight, spacing, and direction.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Use an upright when the task is to mark a place, not to enclose it.
  * Use a set of uprights when the task is to outline a field that must stay open to the sky or to adjacent space.
  * Give spanning members a clear support-to-support path. Do not ask a line to carry load if it is only a joint or a frame.
  * Align major elements to an imagined axis before adding local exceptions. The axis should remain readable after the exceptions.
  * Organize repetitive rooms along a path when sequence matters. Let the long form turn or step where the site demands it, without losing the path.
  * To change surface texture, change the weight, spacing, or direction of the linear pattern. One change at a time keeps the surface legible.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Tag each linear instance: `marker`, `outline`, `span`, `frame`, `axis`, `path`, or `surfaceArticulation`.
  * An axis is a construction line through two anchor points, stored even when no solid occupies it. Symmetry checks measure mirror distance to that line.
  * A transparent field is the convex hull of upright endpoints, flagged `void`, not meshed as a solid.
  * A structural line must report span vector, support endpoints, and the overhead plane it carries. A surface articulation reports weight, spacing, and direction only.

#### Concept 6: Line Becoming Plane

* **Definition:** Parallel lines imply a plane. As they multiply and close up, the implied membrane becomes a built surface and the gaps become interruptions in that surface.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Two parallel lines can carry a transparent spatial membrane. The closer they are, the stronger the planar reading.
  * A series of parallel lines reinforces the same plane. Extended along it, they turn the implication into a real surface.
  * Densification runs from a round column, to a square pier engaged with the wall, to a pilaster that is only a relief on the wall. A row of columns is a wall opened at intervals.
  * A colonnade (columns plus an entablature) is a public face: easy to enter, partly sheltered, and a semi-transparent screen that unifies forms behind it.
  * Column rows also bound spatial zones that remain continuous with adjacent space, and they can measure that space by their rhythm.
  * A trellis or pergola gives outdoor enclosure while admitting filtered light and air. Vertical and horizontal lines together can define a volume whose form comes only from the line configuration.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * To suggest a plane without building one, place two or more parallel lines and tighten their spacing until the membrane reads.
  * Use a colonnade when the face must be entered, must shelter, and must unify what stands behind it. Do not use it when the face must seal climate or privacy.
  * Increase solidity in order: freestanding column, engaged pier, pilaster, solid wall. Skip a step only when the change of porosity is meant to be abrupt.
  * Let a column row measure space. Keep the bay rhythm regular unless a deliberate interruption marks an entrance or a crossing.
  * For light outdoor enclosure, use a trellis rather than a wall. For a volume made only of lines, require both vertical and horizontal members so the cage has edges in all three dimensions.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Model a column row as one plane plus a porosity value, not as unrelated sticks. Porosity = opening area / gross plane area.
  * Solidity sequence: column (high porosity), pier (medium), pilaster (low, carved from a solid plane), wall (porosity near 0).
  * Implied-plane strength increases as spacing decreases and as the lines' overlap along their length increases. When porosity drops under a threshold, replace the line array with a solid face punctured by openings.
  * A line-defined volume exists when the cage has extents on three axes and no face is required to be opaque.

#### Concept 7: Plane

* **Definition:** A plane is a line extended in a direction other than its own. It has length and width and no depth. In architecture it is the element that sets the boundary of a volume.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Shape, fixed by the contour of the edge, is the primary identity of a plane.
  * Perspective foreshortening distorts shape. The true shape appears only in a frontal view.
  * Surface color, pattern, and texture change visual weight and apparent stability.
  * Size, shape, color, texture, and the relationship among several planes determine both the form they describe and the space they enclose.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Decide the edge contour before decorating the surface. Shape identifies the plane; surface only qualifies it.
  * Judge a plane's shape in a frontal view (plan for a floor, elevation for a wall). Do not confirm shape from an oblique view.
  * Darken, coarsen, or heavily pattern a surface to increase visual weight. Lighten and quiet it when the plane should recede.
  * Every volume in the model must be traceable to the planes that bound it. A plane that bounds nothing is an incomplete move.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * A plane record stores edge loop (shape), normal (orientation), surface attributes (color, pattern, texture), and position.
  * True-shape test: the viewing direction is anti-parallel to the normal. Any other view is flagged foreshortened and is not used to measure shape.
  * Visual weight is a scalar from surface attributes, separate from geometric area.
  * A plane is valid in this chapter when it is referenced by at least one volume as a boundary face.

#### Concept 8: Three Generic Planes

* **Definition:** Architectural planes fall into three families by orientation and role: the overhead plane, the wall plane, and the base plane.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Overhead plane: the roof that shelters interiors from weather, or the ceiling that closes a room from above.
  * Wall plane: vertical, active in ordinary vision, and the main shaper of enclosure.
  * Base plane: the ground that physically and visually supports the building, or the floor that is walked on.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Classify every surface into one family before detailing it. A tilted plane must be assigned by its dominant role (shelter, enclosure, or support), not only by a midpoint angle.
  * Do not ask the floor to do the wall's job of shaping the view, or the ceiling to do the floor's job of carrying use, without an explicit exception.
  * The wall is the default instrument of enclosure because it stands in the field of vision. Overhead and base modify that enclosure from above and below.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Classify by normal and elevation. Near-horizontal and low: `base`. Near-vertical: `wall`. Near-horizontal and high: `overhead`.
  * Split `base` into `ground` (site datum) and `floor` (walkable finish). Split `overhead` into `roof` (weather plane) and `ceiling` (room lining).
  * Store the class on the face. Downstream rules for ground, floor, wall, ceiling, and roof dispatch on that tag.

#### Concept 9: Ground and Floor

* **Definition:** The ground plane supports all construction and, with climate and topography, conditions the form that rises from it. The floor plane is the walkable horizontal surface, either a finish on the ground or a raised plane spanning between supports.
* **Key Properties:**
  * A building may merge with the ground, rest on it, or lift above it.
  * The ground itself can become a podium, rise to mark a significant place, berm to shape outdoor rooms or to buffer a nuisance, be carved or terraced into a platform, or be stepped so a change of level can be climbed.
  * Floor texture and density affect both the sound of the room and the feel of walking.
  * Floor shape, color, and pattern either draw a spatial boundary or unify parts of a room.
  * A floor can step or terrace to bring scale down to the body and to make platforms for sitting, viewing, or performance. It can rise to mark an honorific place. It can stay neutral so other elements read as figures against it.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Choose one primary ground relationship: merge, rest, or lift. Mixing all three on one building needs a clear reason at each change.
  * Shape the ground before shaping the object on it when the site's slope or a required outdoor room is the stronger fact.
  * Use steps and terraces to absorb grade and to reduce a large floor to human increments. Use a raised floor platform when a place must be set apart.
  * A patterned floor should either bind the room into one figure or mark a zone. Avoid a pattern that does both at once.
  * Keep the floor visually quiet when walls, furniture, or a point-element must dominate.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Site input is a ground mesh plus a finished-floor datum. Building-to-ground state is `merge`, `rest`, or `elevate` from the signed distance between the lowest occupied floor and the ground mesh.
  * Ground operations are booleans and displacements on that mesh: `podium` (raise a patch), `berm` (add a ridge), `terrace` (step the mesh into platforms), `carve` (cut a platform).
  * Floor pattern is a 2D figure-ground map: `boundary` (pattern stops at a zone edge) or `unifier` (pattern runs across zones). A neutral floor has low visual-weight.
  * Honorific level = a local floor raised above the adjacent walkable datum by a distinct step, tagged so circulation must acknowledge it.

#### Concept 10: Wall

* **Definition:** Exterior walls cut a controlled interior out of the surroundings and, in the same act, shape exterior mass and the public spaces beside the building. Interior walls set the size and shape of rooms and the degree to which rooms join.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Exterior walls provide privacy and weather protection. Openings within them or between them reconnect inside and outside.
  * As they mold the interior they also describe massing and image, and in a city they become the walls of streets, courts, and squares.
  * An exterior wall can be treated as the primary face.
  * Parallel bearing walls that carry a floor or a roof define linear slots with a strong direction. Those slots connect sideways only where the walls are interrupted.
  * Freestanding walls, including L-shaped and T-shaped groups, can interlock a series of spaces.
  * An interior wall's quality depends on its visual properties, its relation to other walls, and the size and placement of openings.
  * A wall may merge with floor or ceiling or stand isolated. It may recede as a backdrop or advance through form, color, texture, or material.
  * Walls limit movement and give privacy. Doors and windows restore continuity and pass light, heat, and sound. As openings grow, enclosure weakens, and the view beyond becomes part of the room.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Every exterior wall must state both sides: what interior it protects, and what street, court, or mass it shapes.
  * Reconnect inside and outside with openings. A sealed wall is a deliberate refusal of view, light, or entry, not the default.
  * In bearing-wall construction, accept the directional slot. Cross-axes require explicit breaks in the walls.
  * Use L-shaped and T-shaped wall groups when spaces should interlock rather than sit in separate rooms.
  * Decide whether each interior wall merges with floor and ceiling or reads as a separate plane. Then decide whether it is backdrop or figure.
  * Increase opening size only while tracking the loss of enclosure. Past a point, the wall no longer defines the room and the view does.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Each wall face stores `interiorSide` and `exteriorOrNeighborSide`. Urban walls are those whose exterior side bounds a street, court, or square volume.
  * Enclosure score = solid area / gross area. As openings grow, enclosure falls and `borrowedView` is attached to the room from the adjacent space.
  * Parallel bearing walls generate slot volumes along their spacing direction. A perpendicular link is legal only through a recorded interruption (door, gap, or lowered wall).
  * L and T junctions are wall-graph nodes of degree 2 (corner) and degree 3 (tee). Interlock is detected when adjacent room polygons share more than a single flat wall.

#### Concept 11: Ceiling and Roof

* **Definition:** The ceiling is the upper enclosing surface of a room and is experienced almost entirely by eye. The roof is the sheltering plane that keeps weather out and strongly affects the building's silhouette.
* **Key Properties:**
  * A ceiling may be the underside of a floor or roof, expressing how the structure spans, or a hung lining. As a lining it can stand for the sky, unify the room as the primary shelter, carry ornament, or recede as a quiet surface.
  * Raising or lowering the ceiling changes scale and can mark zones inside one room. Its form can be shaped to control light or sound.
  * The roof's geometry follows how it spans onto supports and how it slopes to shed rain and snow.
  * The roof may hide behind the walls, or merge with the walls to stress the mass. It may be one canopy over many spaces, or several separate roofs that distinguish those spaces.
  * It may extend as an overhang to shield openings, or drop closer to the ground. In a hot climate it may lift so breezes pass through the interior.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * Treat the ceiling as a visual and acoustic instrument. If structure should be legible, show the span. If the room should feel unified and quiet, use a continuous lining.
  * Change ceiling height to set scale or to zone a room. Do not change height without a spatial purpose.
  * Derive roof slope and span from drainage and support before treating the roof as a silhouette. Then decide whether the silhouette is hidden, merged with the walls, single, or multiple.
  * Extend the roof past openings that need sun or rain protection. Lift it when the climate strategy is cross-ventilation rather than a sealed box.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Ceiling and roof are separate faces even when they share a structural slab. Ceiling attributes: height above floor, acoustic role, light role, `expressStructure` or `lining`.
  * Zone splits inside one room can be driven by ceiling-height steps without a full-height wall.
  * Roof generation: choose a span direction, support lines, and a slope that sheds water to a recorded edge. Silhouette mode is `hidden`, `merged`, `singleCanopy`, or `multiple`.
  * Overhang = roof boundary offset beyond the wall. A lifted roof sets a ventilation gap between roof and wall, tagged as an opening in the enclosure score.

#### Concept 12: Planar Building Form

* **Definition:** A building reads as an assembly of planes, rather than as one solid box, when openings reveal the edges of horizontal and vertical planes and when color, texture, or material distinguishes those planes.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Edge exposure is the geometric cue: the thickness and extent of a slab or a wall must be visible.
  * Differentiation is the surface cue: a change of color, texture, or material separates one plane from the next.
  * Horizontal planes (floors and roofs) read most clearly when they project or when their edges are left visible. Vertical planes read most clearly when they stand apart from those horizontals.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * To emphasize planes, show edges. A flush, monolithic skin suppresses the planar reading.
  * Change color, texture, or material at the boundary between planes, not in the middle of a plane, unless the surface itself is the subject.
  * Cantilever or separate the horizontal planes from the vertical supports when the goal is to state horizontality. Keep that separation consistent enough to form a system.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Planar-reading score rises when face edges are visible from the principal views (edge not coplanar with a neighboring face, or revealed by an opening) and when adjacent faces differ in material, color, or texture.
  * A box with coplanar flush faces and one material scores as a solid mass, not as a planar assembly.
  * Record each exposed edge as a line element (Concept 5) so articulation and planar reading stay linked.

#### Concept 13: Volume

* **Definition:** A volume is a plane extended along a direction other than its own. It has length, width, and depth. Its identity is form: the shapes of its bounding planes and how those planes relate. It is either a solid that displaces space or a void that contains space.
* **Key Properties:**
  * Every volume decomposes into vertices (where several planes meet), edges (where two planes meet), and surfaces (the boundaries).
  * Solid: space displaced by mass. In elevation and in the landscape the building is an object occupying a volume.
  * Void: space contained by wall, floor, and ceiling or roof. In plan and section the building is a container. The same duality applies to a court cut out of a mass, to rooms around a court, and to buildings that enclose an urban square.
  * Orthographic drawings must be read both ways: the drawing that shows mass, and the drawing that shows the space inside or between masses.
* **Design Rules (Heuristics):**
  * For every scheme, state the solid and the void. A mass without a contained or displaced space is incomplete, and so is a room whose bounding mass is undefined.
  * When the building is an object in the landscape, design the displaced volume and its silhouette. When the building is a container, design the enclosed volume and the planes that hold it.
  * A court, a carved interior, or a square is a void volume. Give it the same boundary analysis as a room: vertices, edges, and surfaces.
  * Check plan, section, and elevation against both readings. An elevation that shows only mass, or a plan that shows only rooms, is a partial description.
* **Computational / Generative Logic:**
  * Represent each volume as a boundary model: vertices, edges, faces. Derive form from the face shapes and their adjacency, not from a bounding box alone.
  * Persist two solids for the same work: `solidMass` (union of built matter) and `enclosedVoid` (cells bounded by faces, including courts and urban voids whose boundaries are neighboring buildings).
  * A plan cut reports the void cells and the wall thicknesses. An elevation reports the silhouette of `solidMass`. A section must show both in one cut.
  * Validation fails if a face belongs to neither representation, or if a void cell has an open boundary that was not intentionally tagged as an opening.
