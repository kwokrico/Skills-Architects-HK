# Geoguide 5 — Guide to Slope Maintenance (Fourth Edition)
**Architect critical summary for schematic design**
Fourth Edition, November 2023 | Geotechnical Engineering Office, Civil Engineering and Development Department

> Scope note: Fourth Edition, November 2023, signed by Raymond WM Cheung, Head, Geotechnical Engineering Office, Civil Engineering and Development Department (CEDD). The foreword states that, as with other Geoguides, this document gives guidance on good engineering practice, and that its recommendations are not intended to be mandatory. It recommends maintenance of man-made slopes, retaining walls and natural terrain hazard mitigation measures. It is a continuously updated document; updated information is released on the CEDD website.

## Regulatory Overview
Geoguide 5 (Fourth Edition, November 2023) recommends a standard of good practice for the maintenance of man-made slopes, retaining walls and natural terrain hazard mitigation measures, and the foreword states that its recommendations are not intended to be mandatory. Maintenance responsibility for land, including slopes and retaining walls, rests with the owner as defined in the Building Management Ordinance (Chapter 344) or with the party assigned that responsibility; government slopes are managed through systems set up by individual departments; and the inspections and works in this guide maintain the existing margin of safety, with a Stability Assessment used to judge whether a slope or wall meets the Geotechnical Manual for Slopes (Geotechnical Control Office, 1984).

## Critical main topics and subtopics

### 1. Purpose, scope and who maintains (1.1, 1.2)
Regular maintenance is generally essential for man-made slopes, retaining walls and natural terrain hazard mitigation measures. The guide covers inspections and works needed to keep well-designed and properly constructed slopes, walls and man-made mitigation measures in good condition. Those same inspections and works can also reduce the probability of instability where the slope or wall is not up to current geotechnical standards. Maintenance keeps the existing margin of safety. Judging whether a slope or wall meets the Geotechnical Manual for Slopes requires a Stability Assessment by a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer, and upgrading works where it does not.

The Geotechnical Engineering Office (GEO) maintains the Catalogue of Slopes and the Slope Information System (Hong Kong Slope Safety website) for sizeable man-made slopes, retaining walls and natural terrain hazard mitigation measures. The Lands Department maintains the Slope Maintenance Responsibility Information System as a preliminary reference for who maintains a registered feature. Lease documents and owner records are at the Land Registry. A layman’s abridged version is the Layman’s Guide to Slope Maintenance (GEO, 2003).

Maintenance inspections are divided into five categories:

| Category | Who carries it out | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Routine Maintenance Inspections | Any responsible person, with no professional geotechnical knowledge | Basic upkeep of man-made items |
| Engineer Inspections for Maintenance | A professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer | See Section 3.2 |
| Regular Checks of Buried Water-carrying Services | A specialist leakage detection contractor | Services that may affect slopes and walls |
| Regular Monitoring of Special Measures | A firm with expertise in that monitoring | Only where long-term stability relies on measures that become less effective with time |
| Special Follow-up Review for Steel Flexible Barriers | A professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer; other disciplines or the manufacturer or supplier if needed | Only when major defects or anomalies are identified |

Responsibility and how it is found:

| Situation | What the guide states |
|---|---|
| Private owner | Responsibility rests with the owner under the Building Management Ordinance (Chapter 344), or the party assigned it. Ownership comes from a Lands Department lease (government lease, conditions of grant, sale or exchange). |
| Outside the lot | The lease may require maintenance of an area outside the lot boundary (Figure 1.1). Owners can also take on adjoining land by their actions, for example by cutting into it. |
| Natural terrain outside the site | A Natural Terrain Clause can require study of landslide hazards from natural terrain outside the boundary, and construction and maintenance of mitigation and stabilisation measures. Those measures and their maintenance are usually in a Natural Terrain Hazard Mitigation and Stabilisation Works Plan registered at the Land Registry (Figure 1.2). |
| Flat owners | Owners of flats in a multi-storey building should examine the lease. In practice an Owners’ Corporation discharges the duty, usually through a property manager. The Ordinance requires the Owners’ Corporation to maintain common parts, which include slopes and retaining walls. The Deed of Mutual Covenant should define the owners’, managers’ and other parties’ obligations. |
| Government slopes | Individual departments set up their own systems to manage maintenance. |

**SD takeaway:** At schematic design, mark every slope, wall and natural-terrain measure the lot must maintain, including land outside the boundary where the lease, a Natural Terrain Clause, or the owner’s own works create that duty, and name the maintaining party.

### 2. Maintenance management, manuals and records (2.1–2.3)
Owners, or their agents, should carry out regular inspections and works. A slope or wall that has not been properly maintained should start with Routine Maintenance Inspections and the works those inspections show are needed, and the first Engineer Inspection for Maintenance should be commissioned as soon as possible, particularly where there is no Maintenance Manual. After that, inspections and works follow the Maintenance Manual. The Buildings Department administers a government loan scheme for individual owners of private buildings to reinstate or improve slope safety, including repairs, regular maintenance and removal of unauthorised building works, whether done voluntarily or under statutory orders.

The engineer who designs a slope, retaining wall or natural terrain hazard mitigation measure should prepare a Maintenance Manual as part of the design service. If none exists, the engineer commissioned for the Engineer Inspection for Maintenance, or responsible for upgrading works, prepares it. The same applies to natural terrain measures that require Routine Maintenance Inspections (Section 6.3). Where a manual exists, the engineer updates it where necessary at each Engineer Inspection for Maintenance.

A Maintenance Manual for man-made slopes and retaining walls should include:

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| (a) | A plan of the site showing the slopes and walls to be maintained (Figure 2.1). The indicative figure notes dimensions in millimetres and levels in metres above Principal Datum. |
| (b) | Record sheets with general information on the slopes and walls |
| (c) | As-built plans and typical cross-sections, including surface cover, surface drainage, subsurface drainage, access points and stabilisation measures such as soil nails |
| (d) | Layout plans of water-carrying services on or adjacent to the slopes or walls, and documentation of special features such as ducting |
| (e) | As-built record photographs |
| (f) | A list of man-made items requiring routine maintenance |
| (g) | Recommended frequency of Routine Maintenance Inspections, Engineer Inspections for Maintenance, and Regular Checks of Buried Water-carrying Services, including ducting systems, on or adjacent to the slopes and walls |
| (h) | Maintenance requirements for protection of reinforcement in a reinforced fill slope or structure, if applicable |
| (i) | Maintenance requirements for landscape items and the rationale for their selection |
| (j) | Outline of the basis of design and/or Stability Assessment findings, including the consequence-to-life category of each slope and wall |
| (k) | Detailed rock joint mapping records for rock slopes with a hard surface cover |
| (l) | A Monitoring Schedule where long-term monitoring is a design requirement. The GEO requires long-term monitoring of ground anchors and designed raking drains |
| (m) | A list of other documentary information, for example the geotechnical report |

The manual should also tell Routine Maintenance Inspection personnel when to arrange an immediate Engineer Inspection for Maintenance, for example where signs of leakage, suspected leakage, or ground subsidence are observed. An indicative format is in Appendix A. Key aspects of a manual for natural terrain measures are in Chapter 6. Engineers should review previous Stability Assessments or upgrading designs before putting them in the manual, and should append superseded records that explain the history. For features checked by the Building Authority, the inspecting engineer may approach the Buildings Department for construction drawings and design details. GEO Stability Assessment reports and designs of landslip preventive measures and natural terrain hazard mitigation measures are in the Civil Engineering Library of CEDD.

The owner, the party required to maintain the land, or the appointed agent keeps the Maintenance Manual and all inspection and works records. Duplicate copies in separate locations are advisable. Where many slopes and walls are maintained, electronic copies should be considered.

**SD takeaway:** Include the Maintenance Manual in the design-service scope, with as-built geometry, access points, services layouts, the consequence-to-life category and the Monitoring Schedule, and state who will keep duplicate records after handover.

### 3. Routine Maintenance Inspections (3.1.1–3.1.6)
As a minimum, Routine Maintenance Inspections check the need to maintain man-made items: clear debris from channels and the slope surface; repair cracked or damaged channels or pavement; repair or replace cracked or damaged surface cover; unblock weepholes and outlet drainpipes; remove vegetation that has severely cracked cover or channels; re-grass bare soil; repair missing or deteriorated pointing in masonry walls; remove loose rock debris and undesirable vegetation from rock slopes or around boulders; check for leakage of buried and exposed water-carrying services and, if found, alert the services’ owners, maintenance parties or authorities; repair or replace rusted steel slope furniture; and maintain landscape items. A separate Regular Check of buried services is in Section 3.3. Where the same defect needs repeated repair, the cause should be investigated. Abnormal features are noted and an immediate Engineer Inspection is arranged if Section 3.1.5 applies.

Frequency is Table 3.1. Designers, or engineers undertaking Engineer Inspections, may specify inspections more often than the table, for example where a high indirect consequence is anticipated if the slope or wall fails. Less frequent inspections may be adopted taking into account size, the stabilisation measures and the cost-benefit of inspecting. The example given is small slopes and retaining walls with height not exceeding 3 m.

| Consequence-to-life category (GEO, 2007) | Routine Maintenance Inspection frequency |
|---|---|
| Category 1 and 2 | Once every year |
| Category 3 | Once every two years |

If Routine Maintenance Inspections are carried out not more than once a year, they should preferably be carried out between October and February, and required maintenance works should be completed before the onset of the wet season in April. It is good practice to inspect drainage channels and clear any blockage after a heavy rainstorm.

Any responsible person can carry out the inspection, including property management staff or maintenance staff. Owners may employ technically qualified staff. Government departments generally deploy staff at the rank of Assistant Clerk of Works, Technical Officer or Works Supervisor.

Typical works from Table 3.2, which general building or civil engineering maintenance contractors can carry out:

| Man-made item | Typical works |
|---|---|
| Surface drainage channels, catchpits and sand traps | Clear debris, undesirable vegetation and obstructions; repair minor cracks with cement mortar or flexible sealant; rebuild severely cracked channels; replace missing or deteriorated joint fillers and sealant. Works may be required outside the site boundary so debris does not block the system. |
| Weepholes and drainage pipes | Clear obstructions and probe for deeper ones. Leaky or severely blocked pipes on slopes should be replaced with drainage channels where possible. |
| Impermeable surface cover (chunam, shotcrete) | Remove undesirable vegetation; repair cracks or spalling; regrade and repair eroded areas; replace cover that has separated from the soil; replace joint fillers and sealant; remove dead, decaying or unstable trees. |
| Vegetated surface cover | Regrade eroded areas with compacted soil and replant; replant where surfacing has died; trim overgrown vegetation; remove dead, decaying or unstable trees. |
| Rock slopes and boulders | Repair cracked or spalled concrete surface and support; remove loose rock debris and undesirable vegetation. Trees prising rock joints should be removed, including the stump, and the roots sterilised. |
| Facing | Re-point deteriorated masonry joints; repair cracked or spalled concrete and replace joint fillers and sealant. Continual distress, such as widening cracks, is reported to the owner or maintaining party. |

The table notes that safe and efficient access is important for the works. The Buildings Department and District Offices hold a list of Registered Contractors who have indicated willingness to carry out slope maintenance. For minor surface erosion on consequence-to-life Category 3 slopes, little or no work is needed if a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer has assessed that treatment is not required, taking account of cost-effectiveness, direct and indirect consequence of failure, visual impact, and whether the erosion is a precursor to a larger failure or further deterioration would harm stability. Soft landscape is normally designed to be ecologically sustainable and self-supporting once established. Routine maintenance should stop vegetation from affecting drainage channels and slope access (litter clearance and local trimming). Pest control, disease control and tree surgery need arborists or specialist contractors where necessary. Landscape and bioengineering maintenance is in GEO Publication No. 1/2011.

During a Routine Maintenance Inspection, note anything unusual or abnormal and report it promptly. The owner or maintaining party then appoints a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer without delay for an immediate Engineer Inspection for Maintenance. Printed examples of what to note:

| Observation calling for prompt report |
|---|
| Signs of leakage |
| Spillage or overflow of drainage channels |
| Widening of cracks |
| Settling ground |
| Bulging or distortion of masonry walls |
| Settlement of the crest platforms |

Suspected leakage from exposed or buried services (water pipes, water supply mains, sewers, stormwater drains or their ducting) is investigated and repaired under the Code of Practice on Monitoring and Maintenance of Water-Carrying Services Affecting Slopes (Environment, Transport and Works Bureau, 2006). Examples of suspected leakage are a significant increase in moisture on the surface, or an increase in seepage from weepholes or from joints between masonry blocks. A change in land use near the slope or wall is also reported, so the responsible party can review whether the consequence-to-life category and the inspection frequency change.

Record sheets (Appendix B) are completed in two stages: when the inspection is finished, and when the maintenance works are finished.

**SD takeaway:** Set Routine Maintenance Inspections to Table 3.1, finish the works before the wet season in April when inspections are not more frequent than once a year, and write the immediate-inspection triggers into the Maintenance Manual.

### 4. Engineer Inspections for Maintenance (3.2)
An Engineer Inspection for Maintenance reviews condition and maintenance. Whether the slope or wall meets the Geotechnical Manual for Slopes is the purpose of a Stability Assessment. The inspection’s purposes include checking whether a previous Stability Assessment still stands against current practice; identifying visible changes, distress and landslides, and discrepancies between records and the site; re-assessing the consequence-to-life category; checking that routine inspections, buried-service checks and special-measure monitoring have been done and recorded; re-assessing inspection and service-check frequencies; bringing immediate and obvious danger to the owner, with emergency measures if needed; identifying exposed and buried water-carrying services, including areas outside the lot, and alerting the relevant parties if leakage is suspected; advising whether a Stability Assessment is necessary; recommending preventive maintenance works (Chapter 5); and preparing or updating the Maintenance Manual. Actions on buried services under this inspection apply only to services belonging to the owner or the party required to maintain the slope or wall. A model brief for private owners is in Appendix C. Government slopes have extra inventory tasks in Appendix D.

| Consequence-to-life category (GEO, 2007) | Engineer Inspection for Maintenance frequency |
|---|---|
| Category 1 and 2 | Once every five years |
| Category 3 | Once every ten years |

The designer normally recommends the frequency in the Maintenance Manual, or the inspecting engineer sets what is appropriate. Personnel carrying out Routine Maintenance Inspections may also request one. Inspections more frequent than Table 3.3 should be recommended where appropriate, for example where a high indirect consequence is anticipated. Less frequent inspections may be adopted taking into account size, stabilisation measures and cost-benefit. For slopes or walls stabilised with robust measures such as soil nails, where a post-construction performance review has confirmed satisfactory performance, the frequency may be curtailed. This guide does not state a reduced interval.

The inspection is carried out by a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer in Hong Kong. A suitable qualification is Registered Professional Engineer (Geotechnical), registered with the Engineers Registration Board. Where structural problems are suspected, the inspecting engineer should advise the owner to consult a professionally-qualified structural engineer, for example a Registered Professional Engineer (Structural).

If immediate and obvious danger is noted, the engineer reports at once to the Police and/or the Fire Services Department, informs the owner or maintaining party with a recommended course of action, and reports the danger to the GEO for government slopes and retaining walls and to the Buildings Department for private slopes and retaining walls. Simple emergency repairs that can reduce or remove the danger are implemented without delay. More complex cases need a detailed investigation, commissioned without delay, and interim precautions such as sealing off dangerous areas should be considered.

A Stability Assessment may be recommended if there is doubt that the slope or wall is adequately safe, if significant modifications have occurred to it or the adjacent area, or if significant deterioration is believed to have occurred since the last Stability Assessment or upgrading. It considers geology, hydrogeological conditions and the mechanical properties of the ground. Where upgrading will be required anyway, the guide says an assessment need not be recommended; a slope with a history of failure is the example given, and preventive maintenance or upgrading is recommended instead. Indicative record sheets are in Appendix F.

**SD takeaway:** Allow for an Engineer Inspection for Maintenance at the Table 3.3 interval by a Registered Professional Engineer (Geotechnical), and treat leakage, widening cracks, settlement and wall distortion found on routine walks as a trigger for an immediate inspection.

### 5. Buried water-carrying services and leakage urgency (3.3, 4.9)
Leakage from buried water-carrying services, such as water supply mains and stormwater drains, may not show on the surface and can still affect stability. Owners or parties who maintain services that may affect slopes and walls should arrange Regular Checks whether or not leakage is suspected. If a ducting system is provided, it is checked for water flow in it and leakage from it. Where another party’s services cross a private lot, the lot owner should grant access for the checks. Lease or grant documents sometimes state this, for example drainage reserves.

The frequency is the frequency recommended in the Code of Practice on Monitoring and Maintenance of Water-Carrying Services Affecting Slopes (Environment, Transport and Works Bureau, 2006). Checking of buried drains, sewers, water pipes, water mains and ducting systems is carried out by specialist leakage detection contractors. Methods are those given in that Code. Damaged or leaky services are repaired without delay, without impairing the hydraulic performance of the pipes. Check forms are designed by the engineer who recommends the checks or by the specialist contractor.

Services that can affect soil slopes, retaining walls, and rock slopes with unfavourable joints include water pipes, stormwater drains, foul sewers, catchwater channels and water tunnels. Ducting systems, and conduits such as telephone ducts, electric cable ducts or disused pipes, can transmit water. Water-retaining structures, such as swimming pools and service reservoirs, may also leak. If services are not already in the Maintenance Manual, the inspecting engineer enquires of the utility owners and looks for unauthorised buried services and discrepancies from record plans. Leakage may travel long distances through permeable materials or preferential flow paths, particularly in loose fill and colluvial deposits.

Whether leaky or not, diversion of existing services away from a slope or wall should be considered when slopes in or next to a lot are upgraded, when services are re-laid or repaired, or as preventive maintenance. If diversion cannot be carried out, ducting the buried services or raising them above ground should be considered. The inspecting engineer establishes that diversion or ducting is feasible before recommending it. If neither is feasible, the engineer recommends regular checks and states their frequency and extent. Suspected leakage is handled as follows:

| Where the suspected leak is | Action printed in the guide |
|---|---|
| Services for which the slope owner or maintaining party is also responsible | Immediate detailed leakage check by a specialist leakage detection contractor |
| Services on land outside that party’s jurisdiction | Identify the source where possible; advise the services’ owners to investigate and repair without delay; recommend other landslide risk mitigation measures to the slope owner if necessary |
| Services owned by private parties | Also refer the case to the Buildings Department or the Lands Department |

The Government inspects and repairs its own water-carrying services. Discrepancies between the services and the utility plan are reported to the relevant government department. The Water Supplies Department provides water-main locations on request. The Drainage Services Department holds as-built records of public stormwater drains and foul sewers; the Buildings Department holds similar records for private lots.

**SD takeaway:** Keep buried and exposed water-carrying services, including pools and reservoirs, identifiable on the Maintenance Manual, provide access for specialist leakage checks at the frequency in the 2006 Code, and treat suspected leakage as an immediate repair.

### 6. Access and safety (3.4) — architect lock
Many slopes and retaining walls are high and steep. Dense vegetation can block access. Safe access is important for inspections and works and for the proper upkeep of slope conditions, including man-made items. Readily accessible drainage allows defects to be found and repaired in time. Permanent access alongside drainage measures may be considered where possible. Otherwise, alternative means such as temporary access or other tools may be explored as part of the inspections and works.

Compliance with safety regulations is considered when access is judged, and recommendations are made where appropriate as part of the Engineer Inspection for Maintenance. Guidance on access that is safe for maintenance personnel, visually pleasing, and where necessary secure against trespassers, is in GEO Report No. 136, Guidelines on Safe Access for Slope Maintenance (Lam et al., 2003). For personal safety, it is prudent that maintenance inspections are carried out by at least two persons. The same access principles, including GEO Report No. 136, apply to natural terrain hazard mitigation measures (Section 6.3). When a permanent access to a hazard mitigation measure is provided, environmental impact is considered and a visually intrusive access is avoided as far as possible.

**SD takeaway:** Provide safe access for slope and wall inspections and works. Consider permanent access alongside drainage where possible, allow for the prudent practice of two inspectors, and follow GEO Report No. 136 for access that is safe, visually pleasing and, where necessary, secure against trespassers.

### 7. Regular monitoring of special measures (3.5)
Regular monitoring is only necessary in fairly rare circumstances, where the design relies on support or drainage measures that are critical to continued stability and will become less effective with time. The GEO requires prestressed ground anchors and designed raking drains to be regularly monitored. The designer normally sets the requirements, discusses the obligations with the client, and makes sure the owner or maintaining party knows them. The Monitoring Schedule in the Maintenance Manual gives the recommended frequency, the qualifications and experience of monitoring personnel, protection of instruments, alert levels and the contingency actions if those levels are exceeded. This guide does not print a monitoring interval. If special measures exist and the manual has no Monitoring Schedule, the owner commissions the engineer doing the Engineer Inspection for Maintenance to prepare one.

Monitoring is generally necessary for permanent prestressed ground anchors, for purposely designed raking drains that are not used in a prescriptive manner, and for performance monitoring of other special measures specified by the Building Authority or the GEO. Raking drains installed as a prescriptive measure are not “Special Measures”; regular monitoring is not mandatory, but regular inspections and routine maintenance of all raking drains are carried out so they keep working. Guidance cited for anchors is Geospec 1 (Geotechnical Control Office, 1989); monitoring is by specialist firms. Guidance cited for raking drains is the Project Administration Handbook for Civil Engineering Works (CEDD, 2022) and Practice Note for Authorized Persons, Registered Structural Engineers and Registered Geotechnical Engineers No. APP-51 (Buildings Department, 2009). If results exceed the alert levels, the owner promptly appoints a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer to carry out the contingency actions and to determine whether upgrading works are required.

**SD takeaway:** If the scheme uses prestressed ground anchors or designed raking drains, put a Monitoring Schedule with alert levels and contingency actions in the Maintenance Manual before handover.

### 8. Surface cover, drainage, seepage, rock, trees, boulders and walls (Chapter 4)
Most landslides in Hong Kong are shallow, small failures caused by surface infiltration or erosion in heavy rain, often related to deficient or poorly maintained surface cover and drainage. Inspections and recommendations should therefore concentrate on measures that limit infiltration and scour. Effective surface cover, adequate drainage and proper maintenance are essential for continued stability. Slopes that are moving can deform in a severe rainstorm and then fail in a later, less severe storm. Open tension cracks infilled with foreign material, and displacement of infilled discontinuities, are signs of prolonged movement. The Engineer Inspection also looks beyond the crest, including any steep natural hillside, and notes adverse changes such as new surcharge, diversion of a watercourse toward the slope or wall, or an increase in height or gradient.

**Surface cover (4.2).** Rigid covers such as chunam, shotcrete and stone pitching, and more flexible bitumastic covers, can crack. Cracks are recorded and repairs recommended. Rigid covers are tapped with a light hammer to check contact with the soil; a dull thud may mean the cover has lost contact and should be replaced, and the cause investigated. When an impermeable cover is removed, the slope beneath is checked for hidden tension cracks or movement. Durability depends on thickness, material and workmanship. Chunam deteriorates readily and generally has a limited life; it is advisable that such covers are replaced around every ten years. Rigid covers need properly designed and suitably spaced movement joints. Covers next to trees are checked for jacking by roots. General and indiscriminate removal of vegetation and roots is not appropriate; tree rings should be considered, and in severe cases the tree may need to be felled or replaced with a species that does not have an extensive and strong root system. Vegetation blocking weepholes is trimmed. On vegetated slopes, erosion scars are recorded and repairs recommended. Where an erosion control mat is used, its anchorage must hold the mat, the soil inside it and later vegetation; rusted or damaged wire mesh over the mat is repaired or replaced. Proprietary products follow the manufacturer’s specification. Erosion on reinforced fill is particularly critical: the minimum soil cover specified for geosynthetic reinforcing elements is maintained, and protection of the reinforcement and connections is checked against later works such as laying utility services. This guide does not print that cover thickness.

**Surface drainage (4.3).** Crest and berm channels on soil slopes, and channels at the tops of retaining walls, are checked for gaps in the ground alongside them, because gaps let surface water into the ground. Ponding near the crest is assessed and improvement works recommended if necessary. Culverts or natural drainage lines beyond the boundary that may affect the slope or wall are inspected for cracking, blockage or insufficient capacity. Stockpiling, littering and topography outside the site can concentrate flow and cause washout or landslides. Repeated erosion downslope can mean channels that are too small or a poor layout. Drains with sharp bends, or several channels converging on one catchpit, often spill. Preventive works enlarge channels, ease sharp bends or add buffer walls where necessary. A large catchment is partitioned so runoff is diverted to several safe discharge points, which avoids overly large and deep channels. If drainage adequacy is in doubt, inspections during rainstorms are arranged, and substantial flow outside the system is recorded.

**Groundwater seepage (4.4).** Seepage traces, and flow from weepholes, cut-off drains, masonry joints and horizontal drains, are recorded, including traces that show the highest seepage level, and are checked for migration of solid material (internal erosion). Abnormal seepage or moisture, or a sudden substantial increase, is investigated. Blocked weepholes are cleared. Where seepage appears and weepholes, raking drains or proprietary drainage mats have not been provided, the source is determined and adequate drainage is considered.

**Rock slopes (4.5).** Loose blocks are removed or stabilised. Weaker, more weathered or closely spaced joints are a recurring source of small blocks. Adversely orientated blocks that tree roots may dislodge: consider removing the blocks or the trees. Indiscriminate removal of all unplanned vegetation is avoided. Open joints may need local surfacing to keep surface water out. Where the risk of minor rockfall is high, rock mesh netting and a rock trap ditch or buffer zone, where space permits, can be more effective. Rock mesh netting is strongly recommended against minor rockfall on unprotected steep rock faces of consequence-to-life Category 1 slopes, unless the rock mass is massive and very tightly and favourable jointed such that there is no credible minor rockfall potential. Mesh anchorages must be fixed to the rock slope, not to loosened blocks. Severely corroded mesh, netting, anchorages and wire ropes are replaced. A bottom wire rope and anchorages, if absent, should be provided as maintenance works to secure the mesh at the toe as far as practicable. Dislodged blocks or loose fragments behind the mesh are removed, in particular where the toe adjoins roads or footpaths. Concrete covers to rock bolts are checked for cracking; extensive cracking may mean significant movement. Where rock-bolt monitoring is necessary, frequency, testing procedure and required lock-up torque go in the Maintenance Manual. This guide does not print those monitoring figures. Where immediate access is not available, the face may be viewed from a vantage point, including with binoculars, and, where safety permits, by unmanned aerial vehicle, followed by scaffolding or an elevated platform if a detailed inspection is necessary. Sealing of an open joint must not block the drainage path and build cleft water pressure. A follow-up inspection follows scaling, sealing and tree removal. Local works on rock slopes are marked on front elevations or photographs.

**Trees (4.6).** Unhealthy trees can fall and cause casualties and property loss. Inspections note discoloration of foliage, dead branches, cavities and serious leaning of isolated trees, and specialist advice is considered. Photographic records allow later comparison. Trees are a stability and access constraint where roots jack covers, prise rock joints, block weepholes or channels, or intermingle with flexible barriers (Section 7.3).

**Boulders (4.7).** Checks cover erosion around isolated boulders or rock outcrops, basal and back-release joints, and water or past water flow. Unstable upslope boulders that could strike the boulder under inspection are considered, particularly overhanging boulders or boulders resting on others where the contact is open, soil-filled or dipping out of the slope. Unstable upslope boulders outside the maintenance boundary are reported to the GEO if they are on government land, and to the Buildings Department otherwise.

**Retaining walls (4.8).** Missing or deteriorated joint fillers and sealant, minor cracking or spalling of concrete, and deteriorated mortar or missing pointing on masonry are repaired. Suspected severe reinforcement corrosion or sulphate attack is referred to a professionally-qualified structural engineer. Outlets of drainpipes to drainage layers behind the wall are probed and cleared if blocked. Settlement and tension cracks near the wall, severe cracking, deformation, tilting, bulging and dislocation of masonry blocks are noted and further investigation is recommended. Further wall-maintenance guidance is in Geoguide 1: Guide to Retaining Wall Design (GEO, 2020). On reinforced fill structures, gaps between facing panels are kept free of undesirable vegetation, and protection of reinforcement and connections is checked against later utility laying. Further advice is in Geoguide 6: Guide to Reinforced Fill Structure and Slope Design (GEO, 2022).

Unauthorised cultivation on or above a slope or wall increases infiltration and can change the landform and surface drainage (Section 4.14). The Engineer Inspection identifies it and notifies the relevant authority, for example the Lands Department or the Buildings Department.

**SD takeaway:** Treat surface cover, movement joints, crest and berm channels, weepholes and rockfall mesh as stability provisions; plan chunam replacement around every ten years; and keep tree roots and cultivation off covers, joints, channels and the slope crest.

### 9. Overall state of maintenance and consequence-to-life (4.11, 4.12)
The engineer assesses each man-made item in Table 4.1 by whether it can still perform. Assessment is normally visual, with probing or a dye colour test where necessary. The overall state then follows Table 4.2. Maintenance works from Routine Maintenance Inspections and Engineer Inspections are still carried out whatever the class. Priority goes to slopes and walls assessed as Class 2.

| Item in Table 4.1 | Minor defect | Major defect |
|---|---|---|
| Surface protection | The item can still serve its intended function satisfactorily | Maintenance condition has severely hampered adequate functioning |
| Surface drainage system, including channels, catchpits and sand traps | Same test | Same test |
| Subsurface drainage, including weepholes and subsurface drains | Same test | Same test |
| Water-carrying services | Same test | Same test |
| Special measures, such as designed raking drains or prestressed ground anchors | Same test | Same test |

| Overall state | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Class 1 | None, or only minor defects. The overall state of maintenance is satisfactory in general. |
| Class 2 | Major defects affect the function of one or more man-made items. Significant improvement in maintenance actions is needed. |

When re-assessing consequence-to-life, the engineer considers possible failure mechanisms, site conditions, the scale of failure, proximity of buildings and facilities, the likely density of occupation and frequency of use, the likely travel distance of debris, the resistance of buildings and facilities to debris impact, and the vulnerability of occupants and users. The glossary defines consequence-to-life category as the GEO system that classifies a slope or retaining wall according to the severity of consequence in terms of loss of life should it fail. Tables 3.1 and 3.3 use Category 1, Category 2 and Category 3, cited as GEO (2007). The meaning of each numbered category is given in Technical Guidance Note No. 15 (GEO, 2007), which this edition cites. This Geoguide does not reprint those category definitions.

**SD takeaway:** Expect the maintenance engineer to record Class 1 or Class 2 from Table 4.2 and to re-assess consequence-to-life against Technical Guidance Note No. 15 when nearby land use changes.

### 10. Preventive maintenance (Chapter 5)
Where surface cover and drainage on older slopes are deficient, routine upkeep of what is already there is not sufficient to stop ongoing deterioration. The engineer undertaking the Engineer Inspection for Maintenance then recommends preventive maintenance works, which reduce the rate of deterioration. Where those works are planned, nominal support and regrading are considered where appropriate. In certain cases that may suffice to meet the Geotechnical Manual for Slopes. Measures of that kind, without detailed ground investigation and design analyses, are prescriptive measures as denoted in Geoguide 1 (GEO, 2020). Recommended standards for applying them are in GEO Publication No. 1/2009, Prescriptive Measures for Man-made Slopes and Retaining Walls. They can be incorporated in conventional slope maintenance and carried out by Registered Specialist Contractors (Site Formation Works). Typical works for soil cut slopes and rock cut slopes are illustrated in Figures 5.1 and 5.2. It is preferable that the engineer who designs and recommends the works also reviews them when complete.

The Buildings Ordinance (Chapter 123) defines the works that are subject to its control, and the necessary statutory approval is obtained before execution. Under the Building (Minor Works) Regulation (Chapter 123N), certain slope maintenance works are in the Minor Works Control System. Where many slopes need preventive maintenance or upgrading, they may be prioritised by the consequence of failure and their condition, with precautionary inspections meanwhile (Section 4.15).

**SD takeaway:** Where cover or drainage is already deficient, allow a preventive-maintenance package, including prescriptive measures under GEO Publication No. 1/2009, and check whether the Buildings Ordinance or the Minor Works Control System applies before the works start.

### 11. Natural terrain hazard mitigation measures (6.4, 6.5, 6.6, 7.1–7.2)
Measures fall into stabilisation measures on the hillside (concrete buttresses, soil nails, rock dowels or bolts, raking drains and retaining walls) and defence measures that contain debris or boulder fall (check dams, earth bunds, rigid barriers and flexible barriers, including boulder fences). Natural hillsides do not require maintenance. If earthworks substantially modify a hillside, for example by major regrading, it is maintained as a man-made slope or wall under Chapter 3. Maintenance of mitigation measures is limited to physical integrity and continued functioning. The owner is not required to maintain the natural hillside or to review whether the measures are adequate.

Routine Maintenance Inspections are normally not required, unless the designer specifies them, for soil nails, rock dowels or bolts, and concrete buttresses other than those 3 m or higher that affect and are in close proximity to consequence-to-life Category 1 facilities. The Maintenance Manual for measures that do need routine inspections covers a location plan, record sheets, a list of maintenance actions, the routine-inspection frequency and when to request an Engineer Inspection if anomalies are seen, as-built plans and typical sections, the purpose of the measures and what they protect, as-built photographs, and the areas where landslide debris or boulders must be cleared together with access for that clearance (Section 6.2). Proprietary steel flexible barriers need extra information so damaged or corroded components can be procured. Stabilisation measures that do not need routine maintenance still need construction records.

Routine inspections cover the measures, the area containing them and the adjoining ground. Works include clearing debris from channels, catch trenches and pits, containment basins and straining structures; repairing or replacing damaged sections; unblocking weepholes and drainage outlets; removing vegetation that has severely cracked channels or hard surfaces; repairing ground next to the measures if severe erosion has affected it; other works to keep the measures intact and working; removing debris accumulated behind defence measures such as rigid and flexible barriers and gabion walls; and trimming or removing trees that affect flexible barriers.

| Inspection | Frequency and personnel printed for these measures |
|---|---|
| Routine Maintenance Inspections (6.4) | At least once every year. If carried out annually, preferably completed well before the onset of the wet season, so the works can be done. It is also good practice to inspect defence measures and clear any significant volume of debris after a heavy rainstorm. |
| Adjustment of that frequency | The owner may consult the designer or a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer, taking account of a change in land use and the consequence of failure in natural terrain. |
| Engineer Inspections for Maintenance (6.5) | Not required unless the designer specifies them or a special provision applies, for example a Natural Terrain Clause or a “Green-hatched-black” area in the lease. Unusual conditions, such as a check dam filled with a large amount of landslide debris or significant movement of boulders on buttresses, are referred to a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer. |

A Special Follow-up Review for a steel flexible barrier (6.6) is triggered by major defects or anomalies, including severe damage by landslide, hill fire or super typhoon, overturning of posts upslope, slackening of upslope wire ropes, reduction of the height of the principal net, and severely rusted main components. The review finds the cause, assesses the effect on the barrier’s retaining function, and recommends rectification where required. It is undertaken by a professionally-qualified geotechnical engineer in Hong Kong; a suitable qualification is Registered Professional Engineer (Geotechnical). The Maintenance Manual is then updated. The principal-net check uses the “Minimum Retaining Height” specified in the Maintenance Manual. This guide does not print that height. Trees passing through the principal net are trimmed or, as a last resort, removed, because they hinder deflection on impact (Section 7.3). Indiscriminate removal of other vegetation on the barriers is avoided. Electro-galvanised components are more susceptible to severe rusting; hot-dipped galvanisation is usually specified nowadays for main components. Minor surface rusting may be tolerated if the barrier still functions, provided it is photographed and reviewed at later routine inspections. Severe rusting is decided in a Special Follow-up Review, and replacement is an opportunity to raise the corrosion protection, for example by replacing electro-galvanised parts with hot-dip galvanised ones (Section 7.4).

Landslide debris behind defence measures is cleared as quickly as possible so the debris-retaining function is restored (7.1). It is good practice to inspect defence measures after a heavy rainstorm. Early removal is particularly important where the measures sit at the crest of sloping ground, so the debris does not cause instability downslope.

Drainage built on a natural hillside needs maintenance and is resource demanding (7.2). Channels blocked, cracked or displaced can concentrate runoff and cause severe erosion and hillside instability. Any such drainage associated with mitigation measures should be justified against the hillside’s natural drainage, vegetation cover, susceptibility to erosion, the cost of maintaining the drains and the environmental impact of maintenance access. Where drains are necessary, they are located close to existing access as far as practicable. Their maintenance follows Sections 6.3 and 6.4.

A buffer zone, for example an open space, between the hillside and the development, and debris basins or sand traps built as part of the drainage, have no geotechnical maintenance requirements unless the designer specifies otherwise, apart from regular clearance of debris (Section 6.7). Re-vegetation used prescriptively to repair hillsides after landslides or hill fires should use species that are maintenance free.

**SD takeaway:** For defence measures, set Routine Maintenance Inspections at least once a year, clear debris behind them as soon as possible after a heavy rainstorm, and place any hillside drains next to existing access; Engineer Inspections apply when the designer, a Natural Terrain Clause, or a green-hatched-black lease area requires them.
