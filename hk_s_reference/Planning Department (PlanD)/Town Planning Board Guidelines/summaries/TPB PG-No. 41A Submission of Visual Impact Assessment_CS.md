# TPB PG-No. 41A Submission of Visual Impact Assessment
**Architect critical summary for schematic design**
January 2026 | Town Planning Board

> Scope note: This guideline (the cover date line prints “JANURARY 2026”) sets when a visual impact assessment or a shorter visual appraisal is submitted with a section 16 or section 12A application, or for compliance with an approval condition, and what that submission must contain. It is not a viewpoint gazetteer and it is not a façade or materials code. Appendix D is a sample plan of key visual elements; the text extract does not name viewpoints.

## Regulatory Overview
Not every planning application needs a visual impact assessment: outside the circumstances in paragraph 2.3, a proposal that will not likely cause adverse visual impact in the existing and planned context may not need one. Where an assessment is prepared, the Board is being shown the relationship of layout, scale, form, massing and disposition to the townscape or landscape, so that a development likely to cause major adverse visual impact, especially on public views, can be avoided.

## Critical main topics and subtopics

### 1. When an assessment is required, and when an appraisal is enough (ss. 2 and 5, Appendix A)

Need and scope depend on siting, location, setting, nature, size, scale and visual prominence. Professional judgement is required, because the need is often unclear before the design is known. Appendix A is a flowchart. Its readable labels are: section 16 or section 12A application required or not required; comply with all development restrictions in the statutory plan except circumstances (a) to (g); visual impact assessment not required; visual impact assessment normally not required; and submit a visual impact assessment. The extract does not set out the connecting arrows, so the triggers below are taken from paragraph 2.3 rather than from an inferred chart.

In general the guidelines apply when any of the following is true:

| Ref. | Circumstance |
|---|---|
| (a) | A visual impact assessment is stipulated in the Notes of the statutory plan |
| (b) | It is stipulated in the approval conditions |
| (c) | The Town Planning Board requests it |
| (d) | The proposal is in an area subject to comprehensive planning and design control, for example “Comprehensive Development Area” or “Other Specified Uses”, by a planning brief, a design brief, or a Master Layout Plan for the Board’s approval |
| (e) | Development parameters are modified so as to intensify development scale, with adverse visual impact from key public viewing points |
| (f) | The site is rezoned from a non-development use to a development use, with loss of visual openness on-site or off-site from key public viewing points |
| (g) | The proposal may affect existing visually sensitive areas, visual amenities and visual resources, on-site or off-site, enjoyed by the public, because of its nature, scale, location, visual prominence, design or relationship to context |

If there will be no major adverse visual impact, a visual appraisal may suffice even where paragraph 2.3 applies. The appraisal follows the same principles in less detail. Appendix C is the template for both. For the appraisal, a brief analysis of visual composition, obstruction and change in one paragraph would suffice. The level of a full assessment should match the anticipated impact, the setting, and the extent of the visual envelope.

**SD takeaway:** Confirm early whether the Notes, a condition, the Board, a Master Layout Plan or planning brief, a bulk increase, a rezoning off a non-development zone, or a public view triggers paragraph 2.3; if none of those apply and adverse impact is unlikely, do not assume a full assessment is required.

### 2. What is judged, and how the conclusion is graded (ss. 3 and 4.10–4.12)

The primary planning consideration is not architectural design, façade treatment, colour, texture, materials or finishes. Those are for the architect. The emphasis is the overall site layout, development scale, form, massing, disposition and character, and the spatial relationship with the townscape or surrounding landscape. The underlying principle, balancing other factors, is to avoid development that will likely result in major adverse visual impact in the existing and planned context, especially where visually sensitive areas, visual amenities, visual resources and/or public views are affected.

Magnitude of change is graded **negligible, slight, moderate or substantial**, on three aspects:

| Aspect | What to appraise |
|---|---|
| Visual composition | Locations, massing, heights, dispositions, scales, forms, proportions and character against the visual backdrop: balance, compatibility, harmony, unity or contrast. An inappropriate scale can dominate. A well-designed contrast may add interest if other urban design principles for the site are not negated |
| Visual obstruction | Whether views in the foreground or background are intercepted or blocked, and the loss of views or visual openness from all key public viewing points. Blockage or partial blockage that substantially reduces visual permeability, panorama, vistas, visual resources or amenities should be avoided or minimized, in particular for prominent ridgelines, the harbour, natural coastlines, open sea horizon, skyline, scenic areas, valued landscape, special landmarks, areas of special character, streetscape, townscape, natural and built heritage, and the related public realm |
| Visual change | Effects from key public viewing points with direct sightlines, considering degree of visibility and viewing distance. Compare public views before and after, against existing and future conditions. Address likely public concerns such as a sore-thumb development, visual impermeability, wall effect, and neighbourhood identity and character |

Mitigation to be shown includes better visual permeability, greening, streetscape improvement, and landscape screening of car parks, plant rooms and retaining walls. Repositioning blocks or scaling down mass can reduce obstruction.

Overall impact, after viewer sensitivity, resources and amenities, magnitude, extent, duration, any improvement or degradation, the planning intention and known planned development, is one of five levels:

| Level | Meaning as printed |
|---|---|
| Beneficial | Complements the visual character of the setting and/or improves overall visual quality |
| Negligible | No noticeable effects, or insignificant visual effects |
| Slight | Slight adverse visual effects |
| Moderate | Some adverse effects, which can be eliminated, reduced or moderated to a certain extent by design or mitigation |
| Substantial | Adverse effects too excessive and obstructive; significant modification is required to mitigate them |

The conclusion is to be supported by reasoned judgement and accurate illustrations, and is to refer where relevant to the Urban Design Guidelines in the Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines, any planning brief or design brief, the Study on Landscape Value Mapping of Hong Kong, the Town Planning Board’s Vision Statement for Victoria Harbour (the PDF prints “Habrour”), and the Harbour Planning Principles and Harbour Planning Guidelines of the then Harbour-front Enhancement Committee. Appendix B is a process chart: define the assessment area, identify visual elements, select viewing points and grade sensitivity as low, medium or high, appraise changes, then evaluate overall impact.

**SD takeaway:** Write the conclusion as beneficial, negligible, slight, moderate or substantial on layout, scale, form and massing; a substantial impact means the mass has to be modified, not only recoloured.

### 3. Assessment area, visual envelope, viewing distance and field of vision (ss. 4.3–4.4 and Appendix C)

| Term | Rule as printed |
|---|---|
| Assessment area | The area of visual influence within which the proposed development is pronouncedly visible from public viewers. It varies with the size of the development, the site context, and the distance and location of public viewers. A larger building is more likely to read clearly than a smaller one at the same distance. Groups of buildings seen from farther away may be less distinct than a close view of one building |
| Visual envelope | The actual assessment area. It is expected to cover the fields of view from public viewers in direct sight of the proposal. Determine it from the size of the development, its distance and potential visibility from the selected public viewing points, and the site and surrounding topography, verified by ground inspection |
| Initial approximation | Before ground inspection, approximate the area from topographic maps, manually or by computer. Appendix C calls the starting area the Initial Assessment Area, with a **radius of building height × 3** |
| Three times building height (footnote 1) | When the viewer is at a distance equal to **three times the height of the building**, the viewer will tend to see the building as part of a group rather than as a single building. Source as printed: Werner Hegemann and Elbert Peets, *The American Vitruvius* (the PDF prints “Vitruyius”), an Architect’s Handbook of Civic Art. This is an initial reference for the extent of the assessment area, subject to ground inspection |
| Field of vision (footnote 2) | Normal field of vision is **60° vertically and horizontally** without straining the eyes |
| Harbourfront | The assessment area may have to extend to the opposite side of the harbour and to the ridgelines in the visual backdrop |
| Exclusions | Areas where views are substantially blocked by buildings, structures, vegetation or topography in between can be excluded |

**SD takeaway:** Start the assessment area at a radius of **three times the building height**, check it on the ground, and frame each view within a **60°** field vertically and horizontally.

### 4. Public viewing points and visual elements (ss. 4.5–4.9)

This guideline does not publish a list of named viewpoints. Selection is explained in the assessment.

| Rule | What is printed |
|---|---|
| Whose views | Public viewers from the most affected points. Protecting private views is described as impractical in Hong Kong if development opportunity is not to be stifled. Important views to special landmarks, valued landscape features, the harbour and ridgelines should be protected |
| Types of point | Strategic viewing points, popular local viewing points, and viewing points in the adjacent neighbourhood. Points may be kinetic or static. They include key pedestrian nodes, popular outdoor places used for recreation, rest, sitting-out, leisure, walking and sightseeing, and prominent travel routes where the development may catch attention. Local points follow the project setting and views of local significance |
| Eye level | Public viewing points should be at human eye level |
| Coverage | Generally all four cardinal directions, and short-range to long-range views. The applicant explains the selection |
| Where to look for candidates | Chapter 11, Urban Design Guidelines, in the Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines; explanatory statements of the relevant statutory plans; adopted outline development plans and layout plans; completed planning studies available to the public. Strategic viewing points are noted in Appendix C as available on the Planning Department website. No names are printed in this extract |
| Sensitivity | High, medium or low, from the activity and type of viewer and, if applicable, the public value attached to the view |
| Visual elements to report | Major physical structures; attractors such as the harbour, natural coastline, ridgeline, viewing corridors, mountain backdrop, woodland and streams; detractors such as pylons, sewage treatment plants, refuse collection points, ventilation-shaft buildings and quarries, existing or known to be planned inside the assessment area |
| Harbour and ridgelines | If the site lies where views to ridgelines and the harbour may be reduced or blocked, the assessment must show whether and how the proposal affects those views |
| Strategic viewing points on a visual appraisal (Appendix C remark) | When the proposal, as viewed from the relevant strategic viewing point, is conspicuously well below “the **20%** building free zone” of the protected ridgeline on the panorama photos of those points, the remark prints that a photomontage from the relevant strategic viewing points “may not be unnecessary” |

Appendix C marks strategic viewing points as part of a full visual impact assessment where applicable, and marks popular local viewing points and neighbourhood viewing points for both the assessment and the appraisal. Tick alignment in the extracted template is uneven; those three classes of point are the ones the template names.

**SD takeaway:** Choose public, eye-level points in all four directions, from short range to long range, and demonstrate any effect on ridgeline and harbour views; do not substitute a private-view study or a fixed list this guideline does not contain.

### 5. Drawings and file formats (s. 6 and Appendix C)

Visualisation should match the scale and complexity of the proposal, and be accurate, clear, concise and reproducible. Paragraph 6.2 says the materials should normally consist of the following, and may vary by case:

| Item | Required content |
|---|---|
| (a) | Site layout plan, elevations and sections of the proposed development. Appendix C notes that these are usually in the planning statement and need not be duplicated in the assessment or appraisal |
| (b) | A plan of the assessment-area boundary, the locations and heights of the selected key public viewing points, the fields of view toward the proposal, the alignment and direction of any major visual corridor, and the positions of key visual elements. A sample plan is Appendix D. The extract of Appendix D is the title only (“Key Public Viewing Points”) and does not list points |
| (c) | Photographs of aerial views showing the site’s spatial relationship with the surroundings inside the assessment area |
| (d) | Photographs of existing views from the key public viewing points, without the proposal |
| (e) | Photomontages of existing views and future views, including other existing and known planned developments and prominent visual features, from the key public viewing points. State the exact location, height and angle of each photograph, and the focal length. Photomontages with distorted angles, for example wide or panoramic angles, should be avoided |

Optional for more complex cases, at the applicant’s discretion: computer-generated animation in the surrounding setting, and/or perspective drawings. Technical details of the materials shall be recorded and may be requested for checking.

| Soft-copy rule as printed | Specification |
|---|---|
| Site photographs and visualisation outputs such as photomontages | JPEG, BMP or TIFF, **not less than 1920 × 1080 dpi**, aspect ratio **16:9** |
| Computer-generated animation, if submitted | MP4, resolution **1920 × 1080** |

Appendix C also expects, for a full assessment, an introduction and method, the initial assessment area and the visual envelope, visual elements, viewing points, viewer sensitivity, the three-part appraisal, magnitude, mitigation, the overall evaluation, the assessment-area plan, and photomontages of existing and future views. The appraisal template keeps the assessment area, local and neighbourhood viewing points, sensitivity, a short appraisal, magnitude, mitigation, the evaluation, the plan and the photomontages, with visual elements not marked as required for the appraisal in the extracted column.

**SD takeaway:** Submit the assessment-area plan, eye-level existing photographs, and photomontages that state camera location, height, angle and focal length, and keep soft copies at **1920 × 1080** in a 16:9 frame without a wide-angle distortion.

### 6. Amendments to an approved scheme (s. 7)

| Kind of change | What to submit |
|---|---|
| Major amendments to a scheme the Board has approved | A fresh planning application with a revised visual impact assessment |
| Class B amendments under section 16A, where the approved scheme was the subject of a visual impact assessment and the amendments change key development parameters in a way that may affect visual impact | A revised visual impact assessment. The printed example is an increase in gross floor area or plot ratio from concessionary gross floor area granted by the Building Authority |
| Those amendments where major adverse visual impact is not expected | A visual appraisal is enough |

General, non-site-specific questions may go to the Urban Design Unit of the Planning Department. Site-specific questions, including whether an assessment is needed and how wide it should be, go to the relevant District Planning Office.

**SD takeaway:** If a Class B amendment adds concessionary gross floor area or plot ratio to a scheme that already has a visual impact assessment, submit a revised assessment, or an appraisal only where major adverse impact is not expected.
