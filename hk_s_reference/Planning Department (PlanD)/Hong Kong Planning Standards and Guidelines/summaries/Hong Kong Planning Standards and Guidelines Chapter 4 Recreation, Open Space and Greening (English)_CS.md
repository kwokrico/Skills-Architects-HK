# Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines — Chapter 4 Recreation, Open Space and Greening
**Architect critical summary for schematic design**
December 2025 Edition | Planning Department (PlanD) — Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines

> Scope note: This chapter reserves land for recreation that needs special facilities and a town-plan allocation, sets population and space standards for core activities, and sets the territorial open-space standard together with local, district and regional rules and greening guidelines (§1.2.1, §1.8, §2.2.1, §2.6, §3.7). It is a planning guideline, not the Buildings Ordinance. Country parks under the Country Parks Ordinance (Cap. 208) are not counted as open space (footnote to §2.2.1). Figures 1, 2 and 4 to 16 did not yield text; this summary uses only labels printed as text.

## Regulatory Overview
The December 2025 text sets recreation-facility standards and an open-space minimum of 35 ha per 100 000 persons (3.5 m² per person), and it requires the two to be applied together (§1.2.3, §1.8.6, §2.6.1). New development is to be planned to those standards, while existing built-up areas are to improve toward them incrementally, including through urban renewal (§1.2.4, §2.6.2).

## Critical main topics and subtopics

### 1. What is counted, and at what scale (§1.6, §1.8.5–§1.8.7, §1.9, §1.14)
District-level provision means a District Council area (§1.6.1(d)). A check for a whole district is: identify the base population, then the area for purpose-built recreation buildings, then the area for outdoor core activities. The plan is described as viable where sites exist for those buildings and the area available for active open space is broadly equivalent to, or exceeds, the outdoor core-activity area (§1.8.5–§1.8.6). Planning is more often done for 30 000–50 000 people. Applying the ratios mechanically to those smaller populations can under-provide, so wider district needs are to be considered and the sub-district minimum may be exceeded (§1.8.7).

| Rule | Text |
|---|---|
| Countable facility | Zoned or reserved for recreation; meets the space standard, taking account of the site; serves an identifiable residential or worker population; is accessible to that population; and is managed by a responsible agent, public or private (§1.9.1) |
| Private residential facilities | Swimming pools, tennis courts, table-tennis courts and similar facilities in private residential developments may be counted for local needs if they meet §1.9.1 except the zoning test. Consult the Leisure and Cultural Services Department (LCSD) (§1.9.2) |
| Normally not counted | Outdoor core activities sharing a sports-centre venue, unless outdoor space is absent and the indoor facility is dedicated and purpose-designed; facilities inside school compounds that are not freely accessible; members-only recreation clubs (§1.9.3) |
| Substandard courts inside open space | Soccer kick-about areas and substandard basketball or volleyball courts in an open-space reservation normally do not count toward the recreation-facility standard. They are treated as bonus (footnote to §1.8.1) |
| Public housing exception | In public housing with obvious site constraints, informal kick-about or basketball shooting areas and courts of minor substandard size may count (Table 2, note 2) |
| Recreation space coverage | In urban areas, building coverage of indoor purpose-built recreation venues may be up to 100%. Rural sites dedicated to recreation may have a lower coverage. No rural percentage is stated (§1.6.1(a)) |
| Countryside | The recreation standards in this section do not apply to country parks, areas outside country parks but outside Rural Outline Zoning Plans, or the rural-urban fringe (§1.14.1) |

Active open space, in the footnote to §1.8.6, generally means outdoor recreation facilities, mainly for the core activities, including games facilities. Passive open space, in the footnote to §1.4.1(b), generally means parks, gardens, sitting-out areas, waterfront promenades, paved areas for informal games, children’s playgrounds, and jogging and fitness circuits, where games facilities are normally not provided.

**SD takeaway:** Size recreation against the district population, and do not treat a members’ club, a closed school pitch, or a substandard court in open space as meeting the core-activity standard.

### 4. Zones and location (§1.10–§1.13, Tables 5–7)
Three statutory zones are named. “G/IC” (Government, Institution or Community) is the usual zone for Government purpose-built recreation, with up to 100% site coverage and possible sports-related mixed uses. “OU (Sports and Recreation Club)” is for private purpose-built buildings limited to members. “REC” (Recreation) is for large-scale low-intensity private recreation in rural areas. The forms of development follow the outline zoning plan. The statutory labels in Tables 5 and 7 are for general reference (§1.10.1, §1.10.4).

On administrative plans, Government facilities are “G(Rec)”, up to 100% site coverage. The prose and Table 6 do not use the same coverage words for the two recreation categories. §1.10.2 says “REC(1)” is higher coverage of up to 50%, and “REC(2)” is below 10%. Table 6 prints “REC(1)” as up to 10 to 50% site coverage, and “REC(2)” as up to 10%. Examples in the table: REC(1) recreation clubs and theme parks; REC(2) golf courses and marinas. “WRA” (Water Recreation Areas) is for coastal water-based recreation.

| Facility | Outline Zoning Plan zone in Table 7 | Administrative plan |
|---|---|---|
| Government sports ground/complex, indoor or outdoor stadium, sports centre, swimming pool | GIC | G(Rec) |
| Water recreation centre | GIC | WRA |
| Sports and recreation club | OU | REC(1) |
| Theme park (Ocean Park is the example) | CDA, OU | REC(1) |
| Marina, water sports facilities, golf course | OU | REC(2) |
| Rural recreation, holiday resorts, land-based clubhouse | REC | REC(1) / REC(2) |

Location (§1.11): good-quality sites, close to major transport routes and interchanges where possible, with access for persons with disabilities. Air and noise can make a site unsuitable, and outdoor stadia and ball courts can affect housing. Chapter 9 is the environmental reference. Stadia that draw large crowds may need a traffic impact assessment. Coastal facilities may need environmental, traffic and ecology studies. Where waterfront access is required, the extract says a minimum water depth and piers, pontoons and sea walls may be required. No water depth is stated. Hardstandings, workshops, slipways, road access and parking may also be required.

Reservoir recreation must not conflict with water storage and supply, and must be compatible with a country-park setting where the reservoir is inside one. Decommissioned reservoirs may be considered for modest managed rowing, sailing and fishing, with feasibility and environmental studies (§1.13).

Elderly provision has no separate population ratio. Indoor courts and fitness rooms should suit all ages. Outdoor fitness equipment, and tai chi areas with rain shelter and seating, should be incorporated in gardens and parks. Ground for tai chi should generally be level, well drained, firm and slip-resistant. Sitting under shade or shelter should be close to the exercise area. Figure 3 is an image page. The only labels printed with it are fitness equipment for abdominal crunch, fitness equipment for rotatory cuff, a tai chi area with rain shelter and seating, sufficient sitting facilities with shelter, and a fitness station with equipment for the elderly (§1.12).

**SD takeaway:** Put a public sports centre or pool on “G/IC”, keep members’ clubs on “OU (Sports and Recreation Club)”, and do not treat Table 6’s “10 to 50%” and the prose “up to 50% / below 10%” as one cleaned coverage figure.

### 5. Open space quantity, class and location (§2.5–§2.10, Tables 8 and 9)
Open space generally means accessible outdoor space in an existing or proposed built-up area with recreation and amenity value (§2.5.2). The Hong Kong 2030+ recommendation quoted in §2.1.2 is not less than 3.5 m² per person. Section 2.6 then sets that figure as the standard.

| Situation | Standard |
|---|---|
| Territory | Minimum 35 ha per 100 000 persons, i.e. 3.5 m² per person (§2.6.1) |
| Existing built-up areas | The standard may not be met at once or in every district. Pursue it incrementally, including through Urban Renewal Authority and public or private redevelopment, and improve the quality of existing open space as well (§2.6.2) |
| Public housing and private comprehensive residential development | Ancillary open space of a minimum of 1 m² per person for the residents (§2.6.3). §3.7.2(b) says this ancillary open space is primarily for landscaping and passive recreation |
| Industrial, industrial-office, business, commercial areas, and other comprehensively planned employment nodes | Minimum 5 ha per 100 000 workers, i.e. 0.5 m² per worker, mainly for workers. If the employment node is close to housing, this provision may not be required, and a time-sharing use of public open space is described (§2.6.4) |
| Other private development | Occupiers’ ancillary open space benchmarked on the standards above is encouraged (§2.6.5). That sentence is an encouragement, separate from the “should be provided” rule in §2.6.3 |

Countable open space is normally open-air, accessible at ground or above ground, usable for active and/or passive recreation, and on terrain suitable for access (§2.7.1). Covered areas may count case by case: an integral covered component; in a sizable open space, ancillary structures such as pavilions, sheltered seating, kiosks and small cafes, subject to the Table 9 coverage rules unless otherwise specified; and space under a viaduct, highway or footbridge if headroom allows comfortable movement, sunlight and natural ventilation, and the primary use is recreation. No numeric headroom is stated. Water bodies and water features with recreation value inside a land-based open space can count. The open water of a revitalised channel may count depending on design. For beaches, the land area demarcated by the high water mark can count. Pedestrian routes, cycle paths, emergency vehicular access and ancillary roads serving the open space can count (§2.7.3–§2.7.5).

Slope correction when calculating the standard (Table 8):

| Gradient | Share that counts |
|---|---|
| Slope < 1:20 | 100%. Unassisted barrier-free access. Correction factor not required |
| Between 1:20 and 1:5 | 60% |
| Between 1:5 and 1:3 | 30% |
| Steeper than 1:3 | Nil. Not counted as open space |

Classification uses both §2.5.2 and Table 9. Table 9’s function column between district and regional open space is layout-broken, so the function bullets that sit between those two headers are not assigned here. The size and coverage cells, and the §2.5.2 definitions, are readable.

| Class | Size | Building site coverage | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local open space | Preferable size < 1 ha. Table 9: where possible at least 500 m². More passive, sitting-out, for the neighbourhood. Includes public and ancillary open space | ≤ 5% | Within 400 m of nearby residences or workplaces, described as about a 10-minute walk. In public housing and private comprehensive residential development, it may be at ground or above ground (§2.9.1) |
| District open space | Preferable size ≥ 1 ha. Medium sites for active and passive recreation for the district population (§2.5.2) | ≤ 10% | Easily reached from street frontages, and preferably within 400 m of a railway station or public transport interchange. Sufficient flat land for active recreation. Natural recreation areas may be included (§2.9.1) |
| Regional open space | Preferable size ≥ 5 ha. Larger sites at prominent urban or urban-fringe locations, or near railway stations and public transport interchanges. Active and passive, for the territorial population and tourists (§2.5.2) | ≤ 20% | Same 400 m public-transport preference as district open space. Table 9 prints the sentence that regional open space is intended to function as large green lungs within built-up areas |

At-grade open space is preferred to elevated open space. Pocket spaces should be connected, and an open design without fences or gates is described as helping that. Sites should be away from air and noise pollution, using Chapter 9. Covered ancillary facilities count toward the Table 9 coverage, but pavilions, shelters, covered seating and covered walkways at a reasonable scale may be disregarded. For open space inside a private development, site coverage as a development-control parameter follows the Buildings Ordinance, not Table 9 (§2.8.2–§2.8.3).

Public open space in private developments means open space under private management where the public may enter, mainly those on the Lands Department list (§2.5.2(e)).

**SD takeaway:** Hold 3.5 m² per person for the territory, 1 m² per person inside public housing and private comprehensive residential development, and 0.5 m² per worker in employment nodes, and apply the slope factors before those areas are claimed.
