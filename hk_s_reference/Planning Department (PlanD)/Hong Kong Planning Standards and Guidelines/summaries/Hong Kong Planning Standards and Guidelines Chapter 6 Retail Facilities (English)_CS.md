# Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines — Chapter 6 Retail Facilities
**Architect critical summary for schematic design**
April 2009 Edition | Planning Department (PlanD) — Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines

> Scope note: This chapter describes retailing in Hong Kong, shopping preferences from a 2005 study, a three-tier retail hierarchy, two methods for estimating demand, and the Government’s limited role (§1.2, §3, §4, §5). It is a planning guideline, not the Buildings Ordinance. The chapter states that the guidelines are not intended to direct retail development and that planning intervention should be kept to a minimum (§1.3, §6.1). It states no retail gross floor area per person and no retail plot ratio.

## Regulatory Overview
Retail demand may be assessed at territorial, district or local level, normally as part of a special study, by an econometric model or an expenditure-based model (§4.1–§4.3, Appendix 1). Retail development is treated as market-driven, statutory zonings direct it to appropriate zones, and the Government’s role is supporting rather than the stipulation of rigid provision standards (§1.3, §5.2, §6.1).

## Critical main topics and subtopics

### 1. No rigid retail provision standard (§1.1–§1.3, §5.1–§5.2, §6.1)
Retailing, as defined here, is selling goods in small quantities direct to consumers, and also personal services, leisure and entertainment services, and dining. It is distinguished from wholesale trading (§1.1).

| Rule | Text |
|---|---|
| Market-led | Retail development is primarily market-driven. Government intervention should be kept to the minimum (§1.3, §5.1, §6.1) |
| Flexibility | Apply the broad demand approach flexibly so that provision can respond to demographic characteristics, aspirations, incomes, lifestyles, and the distribution of population and economic activity (§1.3) |
| Statutory plans | Zonings on statutory town plans can regulate retail and direct it to appropriate zones. The planning-application system is described as already providing flexibility for changes of use (§5.2) |
| Government’s supporting role | Improve the shopping environment: streetscape, entertainment activities, and facilitating food and beverage businesses. The chapter says this is instead of active planning and rigid provision standards (§5.2) |
| Housing Authority projects | The level of retail provision is determined by the Housing Department from its own assessment, retail experience and prevailing policies (§4.6). No ratio is stated |

**SD takeaway:** Do not apply a retail gross floor area ratio from this chapter; size retail floorspace from the market and the statutory zoning, not from a provision table.

### 2. Hierarchy and 2005 survey figures (§2.1, §2.4, §3.4–§3.6)
These figures are stock or survey results in the April 2009 text. They are not provision standards.

Context stock in 2005 (§2.1):

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Retailing and restaurant sales | HK$260 billion, or about 19% of Gross Domestic Product |
| Employment in the related sector | About 413 000 persons, or 11% of total employment |
| Retail property stock | 10.8 million m² internal floor area |
| Ownership | Over 97% private sector, including property under the Link Management Limited; the remainder owned by the Hong Kong Housing Authority and the Hong Kong Housing Society |

Size examples, not standards (§2.4(a)): Harbour City in Tsim Sha Tsui is given as about 186 000 m² gross floor area. Shopping malls on the lower floors of a single commercial or residential building are described as taking up a few hundred square metres. The extract does not convert “a few hundred” into a range.

The Planning Department’s “Review on Shopping Habits”, completed in November 2005, is the source of the percentages below (§3.1, §3.4, §3.5). Shop-type preferences (§3.4):

| Goods | Finding |
|---|---|
| Food items | About 74% of households buy from traditional markets; about 25% patronise supermarkets |
| Convenience goods other than food | 80% patronise supermarkets; 14% patronise ordinary shops such as drug stores |
| Household durable goods (furniture and electrical appliances) | 84% buy from specialty shops; 7% from department stores; 6% from bazaars |

Locational preferences (§3.5). “Shops near home” means within 10 minutes’ walking distance from the place of residence. “Local area” means more than 10 minutes away but within the general district of residence. These are survey categories, not a maximum walking distance in metres.

| Destination | Shares stated in the extract |
|---|---|
| Shops near home | Food items 68%; general household goods 76%; personal consumer goods 42%; retail services such as hairdressing and medical or dental consultations 44% |
| Local area | Household durable goods 43%; personal consumer goods 30%; personal durable goods 31%; clothing and footwear 37%; cinema 32%; dining 37%; food items 26%; general household goods 20% |
| Away from the general district of residence | Personal durable goods 60%; cinema 56%; karaoke lounge 82%; household durable goods 38%; clothing and footwear 43%; dining 29% |

Three-tier hierarchy drawn from those preferences (§3.6):

| Tier | Role and examples in the text | Location note |
|---|---|---|
| Territorial shopping centres | Greatest variety of high-order comparison goods and of dining, leisure and entertainment. Mong Kok, Causeway Bay and Tsim Sha Tsui | Well-developed urban areas, convenient public transport, concentration of malls and shop frontages, plus offices and hotels |
| District shopping centres | District-wide: household durable goods, personal consumer goods, personal durable goods, leisure and entertainment, dining. Examples: Kwun Tong Town Centre, Tuen Mun Town Centre, Tai Koo Shing, Tung Chung Town Centre | Usually next to a public-transport node such as an MTR station or a bus terminal |
| Neighbourhood shopping centres | Convenience goods, household and personal retail services, and dining, for the local population. Examples include estate shopping centres, King’s Road in Eastern District, and Castle Peak Road in Cheung Sha Wan | Usually within walking distance of residential neighbourhoods. No metre distance is stated |

**SD takeaway:** Place district retail at a rail station or bus terminal and neighbourhood retail within walking distance of housing, and do not convert the 2005 percentages into a floorspace quota.

### 3. Demand method and the only floorspace allowance (§4.1–§4.5, Appendix 1)
Two approaches are described. Neither prints a retail gross floor area ratio.

| Level | Method | Limits stated in the text |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Econometric model: regression of retail floorspace demand on independent variables (§4.2; Appendix 1, paras 1–3) | Needs a large raw-data set, and must be rebuilt when new economic data arrive |
| District or local | Expenditure-based model: available expenditure in a defined catchment, converted to retail floorspace demand (§4.3; Appendix 1, paras 4–7) | Depends on professional judgement of the catchment and of market share, so different parties can reach different forecasts from the same data |

Independent variables normally used for the econometric model (Appendix 1, para 2): local population; working population; visitor arrivals; visitor retail expenditure; retail sales; retail and restaurant employment; retail premises price index.

Data normally required for the expenditure-based forecast (Appendix 1, para 6): population, number of households and number of visitors; consumer expenditure; shopping habits; existing retail floorspace and its commitments; retail turnover.

The one quantitative allowance that is written in prose (Appendix 1, para 7):

| Rule | Figure |
|---|---|
| Retail services | Floorspace estimated by the expenditure-based method normally excludes retail services, because there is no direct correlation between expenditure on retail services and floorspace |
| Allowance | Standard practice is a simple additional allowance for retail-service floorspace at a level of between 10% and 20% of the estimated retail floorspace demand |

### 4. Public markets, cooked food centres, and shopping streets (§3.11–§3.16)
No population threshold or stall-area standard is printed.

| Facility | Rule in the text |
|---|---|
| New public markets | The Director of Audit’s Report No. 29 (October 1997) recommended that viability be the overriding consideration and that every new market be justified by a comprehensive review of demand (§3.12). A 2007 review by the Food and Health Bureau and the Food and Environmental Hygiene Department concluded that new public markets should be considered case by case (§3.13) |
| Factors for a new market | Population of the area, plus demographic mix, community needs, public and private market facilities nearby, the number of fresh-provision retail outlets in the vicinity, and public sentiment toward preservation of hawker areas in that locality (§3.13). No numeric cutoff is given |
| Cooked food centres | Pending a review, provision is case by case, subject to the same kind of factors as public markets (§3.14) |
| Pedestrianised and themed streets | Supported as a shopping environment. Measures named, without dimensions: integrated and continuous pedestrian networks, wide pavements, high-quality street furniture, suitable greening, hard and soft landscaping, and clear signage (§3.15). Concerns named, without standards: supporting food, beverage and entertainment, transport access, and management of temporary booths, advertisement signboards and illegal hawking (§3.16) |

**SD takeaway:** Do not reserve a new public market or cooked food centre from population alone; the text requires a case-by-case viability check.
