# Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines — Chapter 11 Urban Design Guidelines
**Architect critical summary for schematic design**
December 2025 Edition | Planning Department (PlanD) — Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines

> Scope note: These Urban Design Guidelines (UDG) are a best practice guide, not regulatory requirements. They lead a thinking process for responsive urban design. Innovation is encouraged. They are not a one-size-fits-all code, and the project team decides which guidelines are relevant (Preface, Purposes, Structure, Applying the Guidelines).

## Regulatory Overview
The December 2025 edition was restructured and reformatted from the November 2015 edition and, where appropriate, assimilates the climate mitigation and adaptation, liveability and sustainability directions of Hong Kong 2030+: Towards a Planning Vision and Strategy Transcending 2030 (Preface, Relationship to Other Documents). The guidelines are a reference manual for new development and redevelopment and may be incorporated into statutory and administrative mechanisms where necessary; they do not themselves print podium heights, building setback depths, footpath widths or site-permeability percentages (Purposes, Applying the Guidelines, Guidelines 1.1–4.3).

## Critical main topics and subtopics

### 1. How to use the four principles (Preface, Structure, Applying the Guidelines, Relationship to Other Documents)
| Item | Rule in this chapter |
|---|---|
| Vision | A city that puts people first in the design of the built environment while embracing its distinctive natural assets and unique neighbourhood characters |
| Four principles | 1 Responding to Context; 2 Pedestrian-Friendly Movement Network; 3 Engaging Public Realm; 4 People-Centric Building Design. Each principle has objectives, guidelines, and tips that point to other documents |
| Application | Applicable to projects of different scale and nature. Context study shapes built form, orientation, connectivity, accessibility, height, massing, open space and landscape |
| Companion reading | Read with other Hong Kong Planning Standards and Guidelines (HKPSG) chapters where appropriate. The appendix lists the policies and guidelines the UDG have taken into account |

**SD takeaway:** Treat this chapter as a design brief, and take any metre or percentage rule from the document the relevant tip cites.

### 2. Responding to Context — Guidelines 1.1–1.3
| Guideline | What the text requires | Quantitative content in the text layer |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 Respect Natural Assets | Respond to topography, shorelines, landscape character and valuable landscape resources, and to climate change. Integrate greenery from building exteriors to streets, footpaths and alleys. Establish a gradual transition in scale and height between urban and rural areas and provide landscape buffers. The buffer width is not stated. Incorporate blue-green infrastructure (BGI) where opportunities arise, including as walkable blue and green links | Protect views of Victoria Peak, Lion Rock and the ridgelines from public waterfronts, and views towards the harbour from those ridgeline areas. Maintain a 20% to 30% building-free zone below the ridgelines when viewing from established strategic viewing points (SVPs). The Metroplan tip treats that 20% to 30% zone below selected ridgeline sections as a starting point, with flexibility for special landmark buildings. Eight SVPs are identified for views to the ridgelines and the harbour; the place names on the figure are not legible in the text layer. No view-corridor width is printed. A visual corridor is defined as a space formed by a street, open space, low-lying buildings or other components that allows a view to a distant object |
| 1.2 Respect Neighbourhood Character | Respond early to built character, including the established building height profile, traditions, culture and heritage. Avoid incompatible development at rural edges. Infuse relevant design elements into new work, rehabilitation and revitalisation. Preserve established low-rise and low-density areas as far as possible. Vary height and design. Allow landmarks at key locations. Include a range of compatible uses | Qualitative. Tips: respect the general stepped height pattern descending from inland to the waterfront; locate tall buildings in central areas of new development areas and descend to medium-to-low-rise at the edges. No storey counts or height bands are printed |
| 1.3 Create Permeable Development | Preserve important views and raise district permeability through layout, street pattern, wind corridors (major roadways, open spaces and low-rise buildings), height variation decreasing towards the prevailing wind, and breaking long frontages. Link open spaces, roads and low-rise buildings as ventilation corridors. Avoid blocking downhill wind and sea breezes. Ensure adequate gaps between buildings. The gap width is not stated. Increase trees and vegetation as far as practicable | Main streets and avenues should be parallel to the prevailing wind, or at an angle of no more than 30 degrees. No permeability percentage, separation distance or podium dimension is printed. Permeability is defined as the extent to which the urban structure or building enables or restricts movement through (physical), viewing through (visual) and wind through (air) |

**SD takeaway:** The only ridgeline figure in this chapter is the 20% to 30% building-free zone from established SVPs, and the only wind-alignment figure at district level is 30 degrees.

### 3. Pedestrian-friendly Movement Network — Guidelines 2.1–2.4
| Guideline | Design points in the text | Figures printed |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 Support Sustainable Transport Modes | Connect pedestrian networks within the walking catchment of public transport hubs. Provide age- and ability-friendly waiting areas. Consider pedestrians and cyclists when planning links to railway and Mass Transit Railway (MTR) stations, public transport interchanges, civic complexes, malls, schools and recreational facilities. In high-flow areas, consider electric public transport and charging, automated people movers, travelators or other mechanised links. Provide a continuous cycle-track network in new development areas, with bicycle parking. Separate cycle tracks from carriageways and footpaths by landscape buffers or kerbs. The buffer width is not stated. Shared paths may be considered where paths are mainly for leisure | A walk of less than 500 m to MTR stations, other rail stations and public transport nodes is commonly considered convenient. Bicycle-parking numbers are not printed; the tip refers to HKPSG Chapter 8 |
| 2.2 Promote Walkability | Tree-lined streets with weather protection along frontages, such as canopies or colonnades, where practicable. Allow retail, café, dining and temporary uses where appropriate. Provide seating, public art, trees and planting. Consider full-time or part-time pedestrian streets, wider pavements, reduced carriageways, dropped kerbs, removable bollards and shared surfaces. No pavement width is printed. Place crossings on desire lines and avoid staggered crossings where possible. Increase pedestrian space where practicable, including widening footpaths and relocating on-street parking underground. Provide building setbacks at ground level along narrow streets. The setback depth is not stated. Reuse service lanes, back alleys and leftover spaces, including space underneath flyovers. Prefer direct ground-level connections, supplemented by elevated walkways or subways where necessary. Provide cool spots and green oases. Set aside space, to the maximum extent practicable, for continuous vegetation between the pedestrian path and the road. That landscape-buffer width is not stated | None in this guideline. Footpath standards are referred to HKPSG Chapter 8 |
| 2.3 Create Efficient Multi-layered Connections | Integrate street, underground and elevated links at central areas and mixed-use nodes, with universal access. Prioritise street-level movement. Keep footbridges short, direct and connected to destinations, and minimise level changes. Divert car-park access to the site periphery. Consider alignment so that scenic views and landscape resources are not disrupted. Avoid free-standing footbridges. Prefer column-free footbridges where possible. Cover new permanent footbridges, elevated walkways and associated ramps and stairways. Provide seating where distances are unavoidably long. “Unavoidably long” is not given a length | None |
| 2.4 Embed Active Design Features | Create destinations that can hold varied activities, usable at all times, co-located with compatible uses, with open space visible and accessible. Encourage spontaneous activity for all ages and abilities. Make walking journeys connected, safe, attractive and interesting. Consider featured outdoor stairs and ramps, and point-of-decision prompts, so that the more physically challenging route is visible. Walkability is defined through four pillars: connected, safe, enjoyable and smart | None. The tip refers to the Active Design Guidelines |

**SD takeaway:** The only walking distance printed here is the less-than-500 m catchment. Setbacks, footpath widths and landscape buffers in this section are qualitative.

### 4. Engaging Public Realm — Guidelines 3.1–3.5
| Guideline | Design points in the text |
|---|---|
| 3.1 Welcoming Parks and Plazas | Keep a collection of open spaces of different size and character. Provide focal points, active frontages onto parks and plazas, and flexible space for exercise, gathering and events. Light the space and design for passive surveillance, all ages and abilities. Maximise accessible plaza space by configuring footprints at ground or plaza level and by providing recesses or arcades. Make public use clear from street level. Public open space in private developments (POSPD) that is not readily visible needs prominent signage. Open-space provision standards are referred to HKPSG Chapter 4; no area standard is printed in this chapter |
| 3.2 Accessible Waterfronts and Water Edges | Provide a continuous promenade with cultural, tourism, recreational, retail, and food and beverage uses. Revitalise rivers and drainage channels with BGI. Create nodes; consider fence-free design with sufficient safety measures. The safety offset is not stated. Use eco-shorelines where appropriate. Place landmarks at harbour entrances, peninsulas or promontories while keeping local harmony. Strengthen physical and visual links, with universal access and minimal level changes. Put vehicular access on the landward side. Vary height, mass, scale and façade. Avoid a wall of tall buildings facing the prevailing wind or the waterfront directly. Connect open spaces, greenways and paths. A tip suggests low-rise, low-density waterfront sites where appropriate, particularly in new development areas, without a height limit. Infrastructure or uses that are not conducive to public enjoyment should, when opportunities arise, go underground or outside the inner core of Victoria Harbour. “Inner core” is not given a distance |
| 3.3 Attractive Streetscapes | Contribute to district identity from the street and from afar. Mark prominent corners. Provide adequate footpath width for pedestrian flows, street furniture, street activities, landscaping and utilities. No width is printed; determine it with utility companies and departments and refer to HKPSG Chapter 8. In older urban areas, footpaths may be widened by building setback on redevelopment. The setback depth is not stated. Keep plant rooms, refuse collection points and loading away from street frontages, and step the upper part of the building low zone away from the pedestrian. Extend the public realm into the low zone with open-air or semi-outdoor atria or courtyards. Provide canopies, arcades or suitable trees for weather protection |
| 3.4 Robust and Sustainable Landscapes | Integrate open space with landscape reserves early in new development areas. Use appropriate plants, especially native species, selected for extreme weather. Provide porous paving to increase surface permeability. No permeability rate is printed. Maximise trees, shrub beds, vertical greening, green roofs and water features. Place vegetated area and open space to windward where suitable, including in enclosed valleys |
| 3.5 Inviting Street Furniture | Coordinate seating, signs, lighting, railings and other furniture. Treat noise barriers and fence walls as part of the landscape; transparent panels can reduce visual impact. Add seating and remove clutter. Design for different ages and abilities, including tactile paving and braille boards. Provide human-scale lighting, especially in seating areas and busy pedestrian-only areas. “Human scale” is defined as a proportional relationship, not a dimension. Shade shelters, including bus stops, and integrate greenery or water for cooling where possible. Transparent or translucent materials are stated to be ineffective for sun shading |

**SD takeaway:** Public-realm guidance in this chapter is qualitative. Open-space areas and footpath widths are left to HKPSG Chapters 4 and 8.

### 5. People-centric Building Design — Guidelines 4.1–4.3
| Guideline | Design points in the text | Figures printed |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 Human Scale Emphasis | Place extraordinarily tall and massive buildings where they support city form and legibility, and give them public functions of city or district significance. Use lower buildings as interface and relief. Break up the podium bulk of tall buildings on extensive sites and reduce site coverage of the low zone. Step back upper floors of the podium or building low zone. Vary height, massing and the expression of the low zone and the tower. Provide awnings or canopies and active frontages. Avoid continuous walls on visible façades. The glossary defines the building low zone as, in general, the lowest few storeys of a high-rise building with a direct relationship to the street. No podium height, site-coverage percentage or step-back depth is printed. A mega tower is defined as an extraordinarily tall building that stands out from high-rise clusters, without a height threshold | None |
| 4.2 Urban Heritage Regeneration | Adaptive re-use should safeguard cultural significance. Extend conservation beyond a single building to a street or neighbourhood. Consider conversion of older buildings with local significance or historic fabric. Regeneration is not limited to structures with heritage status. Protect the setting of a historic building or feature and open up views where possible. No view width is printed. New neighbouring heights should generally respect, and if necessary be lowered towards, the heritage feature. No lowered height is printed. Terraced and landscaped podia may mediate scale. Emphasise the lower floors in scale, proportions, colour and materials | None |
| 4.3 Sustainable Built Environment | Use materials that cool façades, roofs and hard landscape, and sustainable materials such as recycled timbers. Avoid high-reflectance façade materials. Create permeable buildings by one or more of: setbacks parallel to the prevailing wind; open space on large sites; voids in the façade facing the prevailing wind; reduced podium site coverage at grade. Align blocks with the prevailing wind where possible. Use stepped heights and forms that bring air downward. A terraced podium, or a tower on the podium edge facing the wind, may direct air to street level. Provide adequately wide gaps perpendicular to the prevailing wind, and ground-level void decks, elevated voids or urban windows. An urban window is defined as a high-level void giving visual and air permeability, without a size. Encourage green roofs, vertical greening, sky gardens, podium gardens, colonnades or cantilevered podia. Add shading devices and maximise landscape at the low zone. Create naturally ventilated spaces, fitness and art amenities, and biophilic design | The angle between the axis of the building blocks and the prevailing wind should be within 30 degrees. Setback depth, gap width, void size and podium height are not printed. The tip refers to Practice Note for Authorized Persons APP-152, Sustainable Building Design Guidelines, for the relevant requirements, and to Technical Circular No. 1/2006 where an air ventilation assessment is to be conducted |

**SD takeaway:** Do not assign a podium height, setback or permeability percentage from this chapter. The only building-level figure printed in Guideline 4.3 is the 30-degree wind alignment.
