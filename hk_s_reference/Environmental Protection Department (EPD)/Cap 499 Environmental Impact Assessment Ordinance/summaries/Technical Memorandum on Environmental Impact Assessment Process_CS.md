> Scope note: This memorandum is the criteria document the Director of Environmental Protection uses for a project profile, an environmental impact assessment (EIA) study brief and report, environmental acceptability, a direct application for an environmental permit (EP), material change, issue of permits, and environmental monitoring and audit (EM&A). It is not Cap. 499 itself, and it does not print the numerical air quality objectives of the Air Pollution Control Ordinance (Cap. 311) or the Water Quality Objectives of each Water Control Zone. Guidelines annexes 12 to 19 are not copied in full; only the hard criteria and the schematic-design gates are taken from them.

## Regulatory Overview

Section 1.2.1 sets principles, procedures, guidelines, requirements and criteria for the technical content of a project profile, of an EIA study brief or EIA report, for deciding whether a designated project is environmentally acceptable, whether a report meets the study brief, whether a direct EP application under section 5(9), (10) or (11) of the Ordinance is permitted, for resolving conflicts on brief and report content, for taking advice from other authorities, for deciding what is a material change, addition or alteration, for the issue of EPs, and for EM&A requirements as permit conditions. The Director is the Director of Environmental Protection and is guided by this memorandum on matters under sections 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 13 and 14 of the Ordinance, following it according to the circumstances of a case.

**SD takeaway:** The profile and the EIA description of layout, method and mitigation become the material the EP is granted against; a later drawing that departs from them is a material-change question, not a coordination note.

### 2. Material change (s. 6)

The Ordinance definition of “material change” is used. It refers only to significant changes which result in an adverse environmental impact. An environmental impact is considered adverse if any factor listed in Annex 3 applies and the criteria in Annexes 4 to 10 may be violated.

Changes under the following circumstances, without additional mitigation measures in place, are regarded as material changes to a designated project (s. 6.1):

| Case | Printed test |
|---|---|
| (a) | A change to physical alignment, layout or design causing an adverse environmental impact likely to affect an existing or planned community, ecologically important areas, or sites of cultural heritage |
| (b) | A physical change increasing the extent of reclamation or dredging affecting water flow or quality likely to adversely affect ecologically important areas, or disrupting sites of cultural heritage |
| (c) | An increase in pollution emissions or discharges or waste generation likely to violate guidelines or criteria in this memorandum |
| (d) | An increase in throughput or scale leading to physical additions or alterations likely to violate the guidelines or criteria |
| (e) | A change resulting in physical works likely to adversely affect a rare, endangered or protected species, an important ecological habitat, or a site of cultural heritage |

For a project that already has an EP, the environmental impact is considered materially changed if the environmental requirements set out in the EIA report, including documents submitted under the Ordinance for that report, may be exceeded or violated even with the mitigation measures in place (s. 6.2).

**SD takeaway:** Alignment, layout, reclamation or dredging extent, emissions, throughput and works on habitat or cultural heritage are the schematic moves that this memorandum treats as material changes if they can breach Annexes 4 to 10.

**Noise sensitive receivers (Annex 13, s. 3.1),** existing, committed and planned, could be but are not limited to: all domestic premises; temporary housing accommodation; hostels; convalescent homes; homes for the aged; educational institutions including kindergartens and nurseries; places of public worship; courts of law; hospitals; medical clinics; and any other premises the Director considers to have similar sensitivity.

**Design sequence when criteria are exceeded (Annex 13, s. 6).** Direct measures to be considered include treatment of source, low-noise road surfacing, quieter construction method and equipment, alternative land-use arrangement, setback of buildings, screening by noise-tolerant buildings, noise barrier or enclosure, decking over, extended podium, building orientation, architectural features, acoustic windows or balconies, and special building design. After direct measures are exhausted, window insulation and air-conditioning is described as often the last resort for aircraft, road traffic and helicopter noise, because it deprives receivers of outdoor activities and an open-window life. If overriding constraints prevent full compliance with Annex 5, the proportion of receivers protected by measures at source, path or building layout shall be maximized. Remaining unprotected receivers shall be insulated with the Table 2 window types, and the acoustic insulation shall also require air-conditioning because of the warm and humid climate. Where a direct façade measure is used, mitigated noise levels shall take into account its noise-reduction performance.

Other intact gates in Annex 13: fixed-noise quantitative assessment is submitted at pre-tender stage, if any, and before commencement, unless the Director agrees otherwise. Construction noise is demonstrated qualitatively in the EIA by quieter method and equipment, then a quantitative assessment with implementation details is submitted at pre-tender stage and before commencement, unless otherwise agreed. Whether a Construction Noise Permit would be issued depends on the Noise Control Ordinance technical memoranda, not on the EIA process. Aircraft and helicopter methodology is agreed with the Director in consultation with the Director-General of Civil Aviation. For Schedule 2 Part I items B.1 and B.2, noise sensitive receivers under or close to the aircraft flight paths of Hong Kong International Airport, including those outside the **NEF 25** contour, and receivers on helicopter routes, shall be assessed using published flight-path and route information.

**SD takeaway:** Place open-window domestic, school, hospital and worship façades against the Annex 5 tests, keep construction plant to the Table 1B figures where practicable, and use setback, podium, orientation and barriers before specifying Table 2 insulated windows with air-conditioning.

### 5. Water pollution (Annex 6)

**Beneficial-use headings (s. 1.2.1).** Water Quality Objectives under the Water Pollution Control Ordinance (Cap. 358) are set for each Water Control Zone. This annex groups them as:

| Use | Printed criteria character |
|---|---|
| Aesthetic enjoyment | Not to cause objectionable odours or discolouration of the water; not to cause visible matters on the water surface |
| Human health | Quality of waters for bathing and secondary contact recreation, fish culture zones, mariculture subzones, and inland waters abstracted for potable supply, including limits on maximum levels of bacteria. No bacterial counts are printed in this annex |
| Aquatic life | Do not alter turbidity, suspended solids, temperature, salinity, pH or dissolved oxygen so as to cause unacceptable impact; control nutrient inputs to help reduce eutrophication or excessive algal growth; prevent toxic substances reaching unacceptable levels |
| Industrial use | Prevent deleterious chemicals, floatables and settleable matters affecting uses such as cooling systems and flushing water supply |

**Mixing zone (ss. 1.3.1–1.3.2).** A mixing zone is a region where initial dilution of a discharge takes place and where water quality criteria can be exceeded. It generally consists of a zone of initial dilution (ZID), where mixing is by momentum and buoyancy, and a subsequent dilution zone out to the edge where water quality criteria need to be met. Size, siting, shape and quality are case by case. Acceptance criteria as printed:

| Test | Rule |
|---|---|
| (a)–(c) | Shall not impair the integrity of the water body as a whole; shall not endanger or diminish sensitive beneficial uses such as gazetted beaches and ecologically sensitive sites; shall not accumulate substances to unacceptable toxic levels in aquatic organisms |
| (d) Inside the zone | Materials not in concentrations that settle to form objectionable deposits; floating debris, oil, scum and other matter not in concentrations that form nuisances; substances not in concentrations that produce objectionable colour, odour or turbidity |
| (e) At the boundary | The discharge shall not cause the Water Quality Objectives or applicable criteria for pH, 5-day Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD5), dissolved oxygen, suspended solids, ammonia nitrogen, bacteria and ecotoxicity to be exceeded. No numerical Water Quality Objective values are printed here |
| (f) Total inorganic nitrogen | Set to prevent undesirable algal bloom. If existing levels have exceeded or are close to the established Water Quality Objective, the discharge shall not cause any further deterioration by more than **30%** of the annual average levels |

**Sewage treatment levels accepted as an alternative to the total-inorganic-nitrogen test (s. 2.1.2):**

| Waters receiving the discharge | Acceptable sewage treatment level |
|---|---|
| Tolo Harbour and Channel, Deep Bay | Secondary treatment, nitrogen removal, phosphorus removal, and disinfection |
| Other Water Control Zones | Secondary treatment, nitrogen removal, and disinfection |

Printed notes: other treatment levels can be proposed subject to feasibility investigation and water quality assessment. Phosphorus removal shall not be applied for temporary discharges into Deep Bay and Tolo Harbour, for example due to maintenance of effluent export systems. Nitrogen-removal target: **75%** total inorganic nitrogen reduction against annual average influent loads or concentrations. Phosphorus-removal target: **80%** phosphorus reduction on the same basis. Disinfection may not be required if membrane filtration meets the relevant bacterial discharge standards. In general, a submarine discharge outfall may not be necessary for discharges already undergoing secondary treatment plus disinfection, provided the mixing zone would not encroach into water sensitive receivers such as gazetted beaches, fish culture zones, mariculture subzones, and ecological sensitive sites.

| Other activity | Printed rule |
|---|---|
| Dumping (s. 2.2) | Governed by the Dumping at Sea Ordinance (Cap. 466), and by the annexes to the London Convention and its Protocol |
| Stormwater (s. 2.3) | Control at source and abate pollutants in runoff, including erosion and sedimentation control, runoff quantity and quality, minimizing point-source discharges, preventing first-flush pollution, avoiding discharges into poor flushing areas except artificial wetlands designed for pollution abatement, and filtration or diversion of polluted stormwater |
| Toxic substances (s. 2.4.1) | No threat to aquatic life; control toxic, persistent and accumulative substances at source where no numerical criteria exist. Discharges of radioactive substances are prohibited |
| Whole-effluent toxicity (s. 2.4.2) | Treated sewage effluent should not exceed **0.3** Acute Toxic Unit (TUa) after the ZID and **1.0** Chronic Toxic Unit (TUc) after the mixing zone |

Effluent standards of discharges are set by the Authority under Cap. 358. The EIA assesses whether the proposed discharge can meet s. 1.3.2.

**SD takeaway:** Outfalls, cooling discharge and sewage export are sited so the mixing zone stays off beaches, fish culture and ecological receivers; Deep Bay and Tolo Harbour carry the phosphorus-removal treatment level, and radioactive discharge is prohibited.

### 7. Ecology — evaluation tests only (Annex 8 criteria; Annex 16 mitigation order)

Ecological impact is the effect on a habitat or species due to direct or indirect changes, including light impact and loss of natural light. Significance depends on magnitude and scale and on the importance of the habitat or species. Annex 8 evaluation factors, not a species list:

| Test | What raises significance or value |
|---|---|
| Impact (Table 1) | Habitat quality; species importance; size or abundance affected; duration; reversibility; magnitude of environmental change; regional significance and restricted distribution |
| Site or habitat (Table 2) | Naturalness, size, diversity, rarity, difficulty of re-creation, fragmentation, ecological linkage, potential value, nursery or breeding ground, age, abundance of wildlife |
| Species (Table 3) | Protection status under local legislation and international conventions, and species protected by law in Mainland China especially Guangdong Province; restricted distribution, with more weight to species endemic to Hong Kong or South China; rarity, with greater weight to internationally rare, then regionally rare within South China, then locally rare. Exotic weeds, escaped cultivars, captive species, vagrants and introduced species have lower value |

Important habitat types named in the Annex 8 note, with the sizes printed:

| Habitat | Printed size |
|---|---|
| Mature native woodland and secondary woodland dominated by native species | Larger than **one hectare** |
| Undisturbed natural coastal area | Larger than **one hectare** or longer than **500 metres** |
| Intertidal mudflats | Larger than **one hectare** |
| Established mangrove stands | Any size |
| Brackish or freshwater marshes | Larger than **one hectare** |
| Established seagrass bed | Any size |
| Natural stream courses and rivers | Longer than **500 metres** |
| Established coral communities | Any size |
| Other habitats of special conservation importance | By documented scientific studies |

**Mitigation order (Annex 16, s. 5.4), which is the design test:** avoidance, then minimizing, then compensation.

| Priority | Printed test |
|---|---|
| Avoidance | Avoid potential impacts to the maximum extent practicable by change of site, design, construction method, alignment, layout, programme, and similar alternatives. If significant impacts are identified, modification of the project is considered first |
| Minimizing | If impacts cannot be avoided, minimize by practicable measures such as translocating important specimens, confining works in area or season, and restoring disturbed areas |
| Compensation | Significant loss of important species and habitats compensated by re-provision of similar species or habitats or enhancement of existing habitats |
| On-site first | Mitigation shall preferably be carried out on-site, and well in advance of the works, rather than off-site and after completion |
| Off-site only after on-site is exhausted | All relevant design measures and all practicable on-site measures shall be investigated and exhausted. Residual impacts are then defined and evaluated against Annex 8. Off-site measures are provided only if residual impacts still require mitigation. They shall be “like for like” as far as practicable: the same kind of species, or habitats of the same size, or a demonstration that the same ecological function and capacity is achieved. Example printed: loss of a natural woodland compensated by replanting native trees to form a woodland of a similar size where possible. Off-site measures shall be implemented only within Hong Kong, be technically feasible, be limited to what is necessary, and shall not themselves require a further EIA study. Feasibility, design, method of construction, timescale, monitoring and maintenance are confirmed during the EIA |

**SD takeaway:** Move the footprint, method and programme before offering off-site habitat; mangrove, seagrass and coral of any size, and the one-hectare or 500-metre habitats above, are the evaluation triggers printed in Annex 8.

### 8. Fisheries — evaluation tests only (Annex 9; Annex 17 mitigation order)

Annex 9 rates fisheries impact higher when: the impact is permanent, irreversible or long term; the affected fisheries habitats, fishing grounds or aquaculture sites are a high proportion of the Hong Kong total; the loss of capture or aquaculture production is a high proportion of the Hong Kong total; nursery and spawning grounds of commercially important species are disturbed or destroyed; a large number of fishermen or fishing vessels are affected; or a large number of aquaculturists or aquaculture farms are affected.

Annex 17 s. 3.5 uses the same order of priority: avoidance by change of site, design, construction method, alignment, layout or programme, and modification of the project where very serious impacts cannot be mitigated; then minimizing by confining works in area or season and restoring disturbed resources and habitats; then compensation of significant loss by enhancement and by re-provisioning of aquaculture sites. Measures shall be practicable and effective in Hong Kong, and residual impacts after mitigation shall be stated. Fisheries prediction shall take account of, but not rely exclusively on, water-quality and ecological assessments (Annex 17, s. 3.3.2).

**SD takeaway:** Reclamation, dredging and outfalls that take fishing ground, spawning ground or aquaculture sites are tested by avoidance of the footprint first, then confinement of the works, then re-provision.

### 9. Landscape, visual impact, and cultural heritage (Annex 10; Annex 18 gates; Annex 19 design test)

**Annex 10 landscape and visual significance:**

| Level | Printed meaning |
|---|---|
| Beneficial | Complements the landscape and/or visual character of its setting, and/or improves overall landscape and visual quality |
| Negligible | No noticeable effects, or insignificant effects on landscape with distinctive character or resources, and no noticeable or insignificant visual effects |
| Slight | Slight adverse effects on that landscape and/or slight adverse visual effects |
| Moderate | Some adverse effects, which can be eliminated, reduced or moderated to a certain extent by design or mitigation |
| Substantial | Adverse effects considered too excessive and obstructive; significant modification is required to mitigate them |

**Annex 10 cultural heritage:** a general presumption in favour of protection and conservation of all sites of cultural heritage, because they are an essential, finite and irreplaceable link between past and future; adverse impacts shall be kept to the absolute minimum.

**Annex 18 visual gates that are design tests, not the full method:**

| Gate | Printed content |
|---|---|
| Sources of landscape impact | Direct impacts on specific landscape elements, in particular landscape with special interest, distinctive quality and value; and the overall pattern of elements that give landscape character and local and regional distinctiveness |
| When assessment is required | The Appendix A chart turns on direct impacts on landscape with distinctive character or resources. The Appendix B chart turns on pronounced visual change from key public viewing points, or on existing visually sensitive areas and major visual resources enjoyed by the public being affected |
| Examples of distinctive landscape (Note 1) | Country parks, coastal protection areas, conservation areas, wetlands, areas of high landscape value, scenic spots, hilltops, ridgeline, rivers, mature woodlands, special water features, nature reserves, sites of special scientific interest, historic landscapes, sites of cultural heritage, sites with an Old and Valuable Tree, a stone wall tree, a tree of particular interest, and similar |
| Magnitude of visual change (s. 7.1.1) | Visual composition (balance, compatibility, harmony, unity or contrast); visual obstruction (condition, quality and character of visual resources); visual change (changes with direct sightlines, considering degree of visibility and viewing distance, to existing and future public views, before and after the development) |
| Viewers (s. 7.1.2) | Type of viewers and value of existing views, if applicable. The baseline study establishes a visual envelope or zone of visual influence, the visual amenity which might be affected, and public viewers who may experience views towards the development. Scope includes key public viewing points |
| Design before other mitigation (s. 8.1–8.2) | Alternative design that would avoid or reduce the identified landscape and/or visual impacts shall be thoroughly examined before other mitigation or compensatory measures. Examples: remedial façade treatment, design elements and buffer plantings; compensatory landscape treatment, compensatory planting, and creation of landscape or visual features |
| Schedule 3 breadth (s. 8.5) | For a Schedule 3 project which does not involve Schedule 2 designated projects, or if detailed information for the Schedule 2 projects is not available, the landscape and visual assessments should contain a broad assessment |

**Annex 19 cultural-heritage design test:**

| Step | Printed rule |
|---|---|
| Significance | No quantitative standard for relative importance. In general, sites of unique archaeological, historical or architectural value are considered highly significant |
| Baseline | Inventory of all known places, buildings, sites and structures of architectural, archaeological and historical value within the project area, and identification of possible threats and the physical extent of destruction in whole or in part. Entry point: Antiquities and Monuments Office, public libraries and archives, and tertiary institutions. Field survey where existing information is inadequate |
| Preservation outcomes | Preservation in totality is a beneficial impact if the sites are integrated into the project. Preservation in part shall be fully justified with alternative proposals or layout designs which confirm that total preservation is impracticable. Total destruction is the very last resort and only with an analysis balancing cultural values against the interest of the community as a whole |
| Mitigation is not a substitute | Mitigation measures shall not be recommended or taken as a de facto means to avoid conservation and preservation. They must be proved beyond all possibilities to be the only practical course of action |
| Design integration | Designs, layouts, external treatments, colour and texture of materials, but not limited to those, shall be worked out for sites preserved in whole or in part. Total destruction, and partial preservation, require a comprehensive and practical rescue plan. A practical implementation programme, integral to the overall development programme, is part of the assessment. Competent professionals must design and carry out the measures |

**SD takeaway:** Test massing for substantial visual impact and for total or partial loss of a cultural-heritage site before relying on façade treatment or a rescue plan; alternative layout is the first mitigation for both landscape and heritage.

| Manufacture, storage, use or transport of fuel gas dangerous goods. Note 1: “gas” as defined in the Gas Safety Ordinance (Cap. 51) | DEMS (the annex abbreviation; the memorandum also names the Director of Electrical and Mechanical Services for hazards) |
| Other dangerous goods. Note 2: “dangerous goods” as defined in the Dangerous Goods Ordinance (Cap. 295), excluding any “gas” as defined in Cap. 51 | DEP |

Section 12.1 says the Risk Guidelines are those in Annex 4 and Figure 1, and that the Director considers the need for a hazard assessment subject to the advice of the Annex 22 authorities. Where the Director and another section 9 authority disagree on EIA findings, or mitigation measures conflict, the Director may refer the case to the Secretary, whose advice the Director is required to follow, and the effect of that advice shall be to protect the environment (s. 10).

**SD takeaway:** Fuel-gas risk is advised by the Director of Electrical and Mechanical Services (Annex 22 prints the abbreviation DEMS); other dangerous goods by the Director of Environmental Protection (DEP); antiques and monuments by the Secretary for Development; landscape by the Director of Planning.

### 12. Contaminated land — schematic gate in Annex 19

For all development and redevelopment projects listed under Part I of Schedule 2 and Schedule 3, the EIA shall consider historical land uses which have the potential to cause or have caused land contamination. The annex lists, and says the list is not exhaustive: oil installations including oil depots and petrol filling stations; gas works producing flammable or fuel gas from fossil fuel; power plants; shipyards and boatyards; chemical manufacturing or processing plants; steel mills and metal workshops; car repairing and dismantling workshops; municipal solid waste dumping ground and landfill.

| Step | Printed requirement |
|---|---|
| If those uses are identified | Site appraisal for potential contamination sources. If sources are identified, plan and conduct site investigation and compile a Contamination Assessment Report for the Director’s review |
| If contamination from human activity is confirmed | Prepare a Remediation Action Plan. The two reports may be combined for endorsement |
| How remediation is framed | Before any development or redevelopment, follow the endorsed plan using the “Source-Pathway-Receptor Paradigm”: source control by removal, treatment or immobilization; pathway control by capping, membranes or solidification; receptor control by changing site layout or preventing access to contaminated areas |
| Naturally occurring contamination, such as arsenic | Consider pathway or receptor control instead of source control, to minimize secondary contamination or a large volume of waste from cleaning up naturally occurring materials |
| Close-out | A Remediation Report is submitted to the Director for endorsement |
| Site not yet accessible | The EIA report may be approved with conditions that the contamination submissions will be made after the site is resumed or made accessible |

**SD takeaway:** On a Schedule 2 Part I or Schedule 3 redevelopment of oil, gas, power, shipyard, chemical, metal, vehicle-workshop or landfill land, layout is a receptor-control measure and has to wait on the contamination assessment, or the EP will carry that assessment as a condition.
